    Lesson 8.3 Strategies for Computing the Determinant

    There are many ways to compute the determinant, all of which are equivalent. In this lesson, I go over a few different strategies for computing the determinant. Depending on the situation, some are more advantageous than others.
    🔗
    The primary method that I recommend for computing the determinant is to use \(LU\)-decompositions. By Proposition 8.2.6 we can easily compute the determinants of \(L\) and \(U\text{.}\) By the determinant product formula, Proposition 8.2.3, we get \(\det(A)=\det(L)\det(U)\text{.}\) Furthermore, when \(L\) has 1’s along the diagonal (as is the case when we use the Doolittle Algorithm), then \(\det(A)= u_1\cdots u_n\) where \(u_1, \ldots, u_n\) are the diagonal entries of \(U\text{.}\) If \(A\) does not have an \(LU\)-decomposition, it still has a \(PLU\)-decomposition, where the \(P\) just introduces additional reflections, and hence the general formula is:
    🔗

    Theorem 8.3.1. \(PLU\)-Equation for the Determinant.

    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix with \(PLU\)-decomposition \(A=PLU\) coming from the Doolittle Algorithm, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \det(A) = \sgn(P)u_1\cdots u_n.\tag{8.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Example 8.3.2. Using the \(PLU\)-Equation.

    If \(A= \begin{bmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\4 \amp 5 \amp 6\\7 \amp 8 \amp 9\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then using Type 3 row operations to clear down,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \left[\begin{array}{rrr}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\4 \amp 5 \amp 6\\7 \amp 8 \amp 9\end{array}\right] \rowA{2}{1} {-4} \left[\begin{array}{rrr}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\0 \amp -3 \amp -6\\7 \amp 8 \amp 9\end{array}\right]\\ \rowA{3}{1} {-7} \left[\begin{array}{rrr}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\0 \amp -3 \amp -6\\0 \amp -6 \amp -12\end{array}\right]\\ \rowA{3}{2} {-2} \left[\begin{array}{rrr}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\0 \amp -3 \amp -6\\0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right], \end{align*}
    so (8.3.1) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = (1)(-3)(0)=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This \(LU\)-decomposition strategy can be used to derive the determinant formulas for \(2\times 2 \) and \(3\times 3\) matrices. These formulas are well known and you are likely to see them in situations outside of this course. I will derive both now.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.3.3. \(2\times 2\) Determinant Formula.

    \(\begin{vmatrix}a \amp b\\c \amp d\end{vmatrix}=ad-bc\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The proof follows from two cases: \(a\ne 0\) and \(a=0\text{.}\) I will show the former and leave the latter for you to investigate. If \(a \ne 0\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{cc} a \amp b\\ c \amp d \end{array}\right] \rowA{2}{1}{-\frac{c}{a}} \left[\begin{array}{cc} a \amp b\\ 0 \amp d-\frac{bc}{a} \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{cc} a \amp b\\ 0 \amp \frac{ad-bc}{a} \end{array}\right] \end{equation*}
    Then, using the Doolittle Algorithm, the associated \(LU\)-decomposition is then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{cc} a \amp b\\ c \amp d \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 \amp 0\\ \frac{c}{a} \amp 1 \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{cc} a \amp b\\ 0 \amp \frac{ad-bc}{a} \end{array}\right] . \end{equation*}
    Using Equation (8.3.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{vmatrix}a \amp b\\c \amp d\end{vmatrix}= a\left(\frac{ad-bc}{a}\right)=ad-bc. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A useful mnemonic to remember these two terms is to visualize this as adding the downward (\(\textcolor{blue}{blue}\)) diagonal and subtracting the upward (\(\textcolor{red}{red}\)) diagonal.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.3.4. \(3\times 3\) Determinant Formula.

    \(\begin{vmatrix}a \amp b \amp c\\d \amp e \amp f\\g \amp h \amp i\end{vmatrix}= aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-afh-bdi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The proof follows from a few cases. I will assume \(a\ne 0\) and \(ae-bd\ne0\) and leave the other cases for you to investigate. If \(a \ne 0\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\d \amp e \amp f\\g \amp h \amp i \end{array}\right] \amp\rowA{2}{1}{-\frac{d}{a}} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\ 0 \amp e-\frac{bd}{a} \amp f-\frac{cd}{a}\\ g \amp h \amp i \end{array}\right]\\ \amp \rowA{3}{1}{-\frac{g}{a}} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\ 0 \amp e-\frac{bd}{a} \amp f-\frac{cd}{a}\\ 0 \amp h-\frac{bg}{a} \amp i-\frac{cg}{a} \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\ 0 \amp \frac{ae-bd}{a} \amp \frac{af-cd}{a}\\ 0 \amp \frac{ah-bg}{a} \amp \frac{ai-cg}{a} \end{array}\right]\\ \amp \rowA{3}{2}{-\frac{ah-bg}{ae-bd}} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\ 0 \amp \frac{ae-bd}{a} \amp \frac{af-cd}{a}\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp \frac{ai-cg}{a}-\left(\frac{af-cd}{a}\right)\left(\frac{ah-bg}{ae-bd}\right) \end{array}\right]. \end{align*}
    Observe that the bottom right term can be algebraically simplified:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{ai-cg}{a}-\left(\frac{af-cd}{a}\right)\left(\frac{ah-bg}{ae-bd}\right) \amp= \frac{(ai-cg)(ae-bd)-(af-cd)(ah-bg)}{a(ae-bd)}\\ \amp= \frac{(a^2ei-aceg-abdi+bcdg)-(a^2fh-acdh-abfg+bcdg)}{a(ae-bd)}\\ \amp= \frac{aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-afh-bdi}{ae-bd}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Then, using the Doolittle Algorithm, the associated \(LU\)-decomposition is then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\d \amp e \amp f\\g \amp h \amp i \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0\\ \frac{d}{a} \amp 1 \amp 0\\ \frac{g}{a} \amp \frac{ah-bg}{ae-bd} \amp 1 \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{ccc} a \amp b \amp c\\ 0 \amp \frac{ae-bd}{a} \amp \frac{af-cd}{a}\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp \frac{aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-afh-bdi}{ae-bd} \end{array}\right] . \end{equation*}
    Using Equation Theorem 8.3.1,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \begin{vmatrix}a \amp b \amp c\\d \amp e \amp f\\g \amp h \amp i\end{vmatrix} \amp= a\left(\frac{ae-bd}{a}\right)\left(\frac{aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-afh-bdi}{ae-bd}\right)\\ \amp= aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-afh-bdi. \end{align*}
    🔗
    A useful mnemonic to remember these six terms is the Rule of Sarrus, which visualizes this as adding the following three downward (\(\textcolor{blue}{blue}\)) diagonals and subtracting the following three upward (\(\textcolor{red}{red}\)) diagonals.
    🔗
    There are no similar determinant formulas for \(n\times n\) matrices with \(n\ge 4\text{.}\) However, there is another well-known strategy for computing determinants, which is a recursive process known as cofactor expansion. Even though I do not recommend cofactor expansion in general, I include it here as you are likely to see it outside of this course and I want to make sure you are prepared accordingly.
    🔗

    Definition 8.3.5. Cofactor.

    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix and \(i,j\in \{1,\ldots, n\}\text{,}\) then let \(A_{ij}\) denote the \((n-1)\times (n-1)\) matrix obtained by removing the \(i^{th}\) row and \(j^{th}\) column of \(A\text{.}\) The number \(C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det(A_{ij})\) the \(ij^{th}\) cofactor of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.3.6.

    If \(A= \begin{bmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\4 \amp 5 \amp 6\\7 \amp 8 \amp 9\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then \(A_{11} = \begin{bmatrix} \rst{1} \amp \rst{2} \amp \rst{3}\\ \rst{4} \amp 5 \amp 6\\ \rst{7} \amp 8 \amp 9\end{bmatrix}\) and its cofactor is \(C_{11}=(-1)^2\begin{vmatrix}5 \amp 6\\8 \amp 9\end{vmatrix}=-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.3.7. Cofactor Expansion.

    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \det(A)=a_{i1}(-1)^{i+1}\det(A_{i1})+a_{i2}(-1)^{i+2}\det(A_{i2})+\cdots +a_{in}(-1)^{i+n}\det(A_{in}).\tag{8.3.2} \end{equation}
    We call this the cofactor expansion of \(\det(A)\) along the \(i^{th}\) row of \(A\).
    🔗
    🔗

    Sketch of Proof of Proposition Theorem 8.3.7.

    This proof is technical and I will leave some details to the reader. Furthermore, I will only show the cofactor expansion for the first row, as other rows can be obtained by a row permutation. Write \(A=[\vect{v}_1|\vect{v}_2|\cdots |\vect{v}_n]=[a_{ij}]\) and let \(\vect{w}_i\) denote the list-length \((n-1)\) vector of entries \(2\) through \(n\) of \(\vect{v}_i\text{.}\) We know that Type 3 column operations, clearing right, do not affect the determinant. Then using column notation,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \det(A) \amp = \det(\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2,\ldots, \vect{v}_n)\\ \amp =\det\left(\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2-\frac{a_{12}}{a_{11}}\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n-\frac{a_{1n}}{a_{11}}\vect{v}_1\right)\\ \amp =a_{11}\det\left(\vect{w}_2-\frac{a_{12}}{a_{11}}\vect{w}_1,\ldots, \vect{w}_n-\frac{a_{1n}}{a_{11}}\vect{w}_1\right)\\ \amp =a_{11}\left(\det\left(\vect{w}_2-\frac{a_{12}}{a_{11}}\vect{w}_1,\ldots, \vect{w}_n-\frac{a_{1n}}{a_{11}}\vect{w}_1\right)\right).\tag{8.3.3} \end{equation}
    Using the additive and alternating properties of the determinant (see Problem Exercise 8.3.11 and Problem Paragraph ), it can be shown that this last expression (8.3.3) reduces to the cofactor expansion (8.3.2).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.3.8. Using Cofactor Expansion.

    If \(A= \begin{bmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 3\\4 \amp 5 \amp 6\\7 \amp 8 \amp 9\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then the cofactor expansion of \(\det(A)\) along the first row of \(A\) is:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \det(A)\amp=a_{11}C_{11}+a_{12}C_{12}+a_{13}C_{13}\\ \amp= (+1)(1) \begin{vmatrix} \rst{1} \amp \rst{2} \amp \rst{3}\\ \rst{4} \amp 5 \amp 6\\ \rst{7} \amp 8 \amp 9\end{vmatrix} + (-1)(2) \begin{vmatrix} \rst{1} \amp \rst{2} \amp \rst{3}\\ 4 \amp \rst{5} \amp 6\\ 7 \amp \rst{8} \amp 9\end{vmatrix} + (+1)(3) \begin{vmatrix} \rst{1} \amp \rst{2} \amp \rst{3}\\ 4 \amp 5 \amp \rst{6}\\ 7 \amp 8 \amp \rst{9}\end{vmatrix} \\ \amp=+1(45-48) -2(36-42)+3(32-35)\\ \amp=-3+12-9\\ \amp=0. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Cofactor expansion is nice in that it recursive, and hence may be easily coded. It is conceptually nice in that it reduces a big computation into \(n\) many smaller ones. One can easily use this procedure to compute determinants by hand for small \(n\text{,}\) and the checkerboard pattern of \((-1)^{i+j}\) is useful for correctly working out the signs. This is the method often taught in multivariable calculus, in particular for computing cross products.
    🔗
    In fact, cofactor expansion is often given as the definition of the determinant. That being said, this method has multiple deficiencies:
    🔗
    1. the connection between this method and the geometric intuition for the determinant is not easy to see.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For large \(n\text{,}\) our Doolittle Algorithm (and variants) are far more efficient than cofactor expansion. The Doolittle Algorithm takes roughly \(n^3\) many algebraic operations, whereas cofactor expansion take roughly \(n!\) many algebraic operations.
      🔗
      🔗
    You should be able to compute by hand determinants for all \(2\times 2\text{,}\) \(3\times 3\text{,}\) and some particularly simple larger matrices. You should know hot to use technology to compute determinants too, both the double check your computations and to compute the determinants for larger matrices. GeoGebra will compute the determinant of a matrix using the Determinant command.
