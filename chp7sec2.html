    Lesson 10.2 Inner Product and Angle

    Consider vectors \(\vect{v}\text{,}\) \(\vect{w}\text{,}\) and the parallelogram they form. Observe that \(\vect{v}\text{,}\) \(\vect{w}\text{,}\) and diagonal \(\vect{v}-\vect{w}\) form a triangle. Let \(\theta\) denote the angle between \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\text{.}\) See Figure 10.2.1.
    🔗
    The motivating question of this lesson is: How do the lengths of the sides \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\) relate to the length of the diagonal \(\vect{v}-\vect{w}\text{?}\)
    🔗

    Activity 10.2.2. Exploring the Length of a Diagonal.

    In this activity, you will use the GeoGebra link nzvwt6ju to explore the length of its diagonal. Begin with \(\norm{\vect{v}}=4\) and \(\norm{\vect{w}}=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Explore. Move the vector \(\vect{w}\text{.}\) It is constrained to the circle of radius 3 (i.e. all \(\vect{w}\) for which \(\norm{\vect{w}}=3\)). Take note of the shapes of all the different parallelograms you are forming.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The range of values of \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}\text{.}\) As you move \(\vect{w}\text{,}\) how does \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}\) vary? What is the largest it can be? Describe the parallelogram(s) that attain this maximum? What is the smallest it can be? Describe the parallelogram(s) that attain this minimum? Is it ever the case that \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}= \norm{\vect{v}}+\norm{\vect{w}}\) or \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}= \norm{\vect{v}}-\norm{\vect{w}}\text{,}\) and if so, when?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The range of values of \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2\text{.}\) As you move \(\vect{w}\text{,}\) how does \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2\) vary? Is it ever the case that \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 = \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2\text{,}\) and if so, when? In what way does the Pythagorean Theorem have to do with this? In what way does \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2\) seem to relate to \(\theta\text{,}\) the angle between \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We see that, as \(\norm{\vect{v}}\) and \(\norm{\vect{w}}\) are left constant, \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}\) varies with \(\theta\text{.}\) Let’s algebraically expand \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 \amp= (v_1-w_1)^2 + \cdots + (v_n-w_n)^2\\ \amp=(v_1^2-2v_1w_1+w_1^2) + \cdots + (v_n^2-2v_nw_n+w_n^2)\\ \amp=(v_1^2 + \cdots+ v_n^2) - 2(v_1w_1+ \cdots +v_nw_n) + (w_1^2 + \cdots+ w_n^2)\\ \amp= \norm{\vect{v}}^2 - 2\underbrace{(v_1w_1+ \cdots +v_nw_n)}_{\text{"Measures" angle between } \vect{v} \text{ and } \vect{w}} + \norm{\vect{w}}^2. \end{align*}
    This term \(v_1w_1+ \cdots +v_nw_n\) measures many interesting geometric features of this parallelogram and deserves its own name.
    🔗

    Definition 10.2.3. (Standard) Inner Product (a.k.a. Dot Product).

    The (standard) inner product (or dot product) of \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\) is:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}} = v_1w_1+ \cdots +v_nw_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    With the symbolism of the inner product, the length squared of the diagonal simplifies to
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 = \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2 - 2 \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}.\tag{10.2.1} \end{equation}

    The Many Notations for Inner Product.

    There are many common notations for the inner product. I use the "angle bracket" notation \(\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}\text{.}\) Another common notation is the "dot" notation \(\vect{v}\cdot \vect{w}\text{.}\) Further still, some may use the parentheses notation \((\vect{v},\vect{w})\text{.}\) In this course, plan to use only the angle bracket notation, but be prepared to see these other notations in other books and courses.
    🔗
    🔗
    Observe that we may express length using the inner product:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{v}} = \norm{\vect{v}}^2\tag{10.2.2} \end{equation}
    So the inner product measures length!
    🔗

    Proposition 10.2.4. Algebraic Properties of the Inner Product.

    The inner product satisfies each of the following.
    🔗
    1. Positive definite. If \(\vect{v}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{v}}\ge 0\text{,}\) and equals 0 precisely when \(\vect{v}=\vect{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Symmetric. If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}=\ip{\vect{w}}{\vect{v}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Bilinear. The inner product is linear in the second input.
      🔗
      1. Additive. If \(\vect{u},\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(\ip{\vect{u}}{\vect{v}+\vect{w}}=\ip{\vect{u}}{\vect{v}}+\ip{\vect{u}}{\vect{w}}\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      2. Scaling. If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\text{,}\) \(k\in \R\text{,}\) then \(\ip{\vect{v}}{k\vect{w}}=k\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Observe that by symmetry, an inner product is bilinear in both inputs.
    🔗

    Example 10.2.5. Using Algebraic Properties to Compute Inner Products.

    Suppose we know \(\ip{\vect{a}}{\vect{a}}=16\text{,}\) \(\ip{\vect{a}}{\vect{b}}=10\text{,}\) and \(\ip{\vect{b}}{\vect{b}}=9\text{.}\)
    🔗
    How can we use this knowledge to compute \(\ip{2\vect{a}+3\vect{b}}{\vect{b}}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Heuristic: Use algebraic properties to decompose unknown inner products into combinations of known inner products.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \ip{2\vect{a}+3\vect{b}}{\vect{b}} \amp= \ip{2\vect{a}}{\vect{b}} + \ip{3\vect{b}}{\vect{b}}\\ \amp=2\ip{\vect{a}}{\vect{b}} + 3\ip{\vect{b}}{\vect{b}}\\ \amp=2(10) + 3(9) = 47 \end{align*}
    🔗

      🔗

      🔗

      🔗

      🔗

      🔗
    🔗
    We have already seen in Activity 10.2.6 that the inner product \(\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}\) relates to the angle between \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\text{.}\) We now make the relationship more precise. To do so, we will use a classical result from analytic geometry, the Law of Cosines.
    🔗

    Proposition 10.2.7. Law of Cosines.

    If \(\triangle ABC\) is a triangle with side lengths \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\) and angle \(\theta =\angle ACB\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos(\theta). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 10.2.8.

    The Law of Cosines can be proved using the Pythagorean Theorem. See GeoGebra ID: ck4w8asn.
    🔗
    🔗
    In Figure , we see the Law of Cosines, in vector symbolism, gives:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos(\theta) \hspace{1pc} \Rightarrow \hspace{1pc} \norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 = \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2 - 2\ \norm{\vect{v}} \ \norm{\vect{w}}\ \cos(\theta) \end{equation*}
    Meanwhile, we have already shown in that:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 %= \ip{\vect{w}-\vect{v}}{\vect{w}-\vect{v}} = \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2 - 2 \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}. \end{equation*}
    Comparing these expressions yields:
    🔗

    Proposition 10.2.10. The Angle Equation for the Inner Product.

    If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in\R^n\text{,}\) then,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}} = \norm{\vect{v}} \ \norm{\vect{w}}\ \cos(\theta)\tag{10.2.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    So we may use the inner product to compute angle!
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}} }{ \norm{\vect{v}} \ \norm{\vect{w}}}\right) \end{equation*}
    Keep in mind that \(\arccos\) outputs angles measures in radians. If you want to find angles in degree, you need to change units by scaling by \(\frac{360}{2\pi}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    The Angle Equation (10.2.3) for the inner product has many important consequences, including the following two results.
    🔗

    Theorem 10.2.12. Cauchy--Schwartz Inequality.

    If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(|\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}| \le \norm{\vect{v}} \ \norm{\vect{w}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Sketch of proof.

    Note that \(|\cos(\theta)|\le 1\) and apply this to the Angle Equation (10.2.3).
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 10.2.13. Triangle inequality.

    If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}} \le \norm{\vect{v}}+\norm{\vect{w}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Sketch of proof.

    Earlier using the algebraic properties of inner products in Proposition 10.2.4, I showed \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 = \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2 - 2\ \ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}.\)
    🔗
    Using Theorem 10.2.12, \(\norm{\vect{v}-\vect{w}}^2 \le \norm{\vect{v}}^2+\norm{\vect{w}}^2 + 2\ \norm{\vect{v}} \ \norm{\vect{w}} = \left(\norm{\vect{v}}+\norm{\vect{w}}\right)^2.\)
    🔗
    Taking square roots, the result follows.
    🔗
    🔗
    The Cauchy-Schwartz Inequality is a useful inequality to bound inner products. The Triangle Inequality is a fundamental geometric result stating that the length of a side of a triangle is less than the sums of the lengths of the other two sides.
