    Lesson 3.1 Matrix Multiplication as Mixture

    We now return to the core idea of linear combination as mixture. We write linear combinations so often that it is useful to introduce a new, efficient, and compact notation to symbolize this process. If \(\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\in\R^m\) and \(x_1,\ldots, x_n\in\R\) are scalars, then the linear combination:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +x_n\vect{v}_n \hspace{2pc} \text{will be re-written as} \hspace{2pc} [\vect{v}_1 | \cdots | \vect{v}_n]\vect{x} \end{equation*}
    where the vector \(\vect{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots \\ x_n\end{bmatrix}\) is the serving size vector (see Definition ) for the mixture and the new object \([\vect{v}_1 | \cdots | \vect{v}_n]\) obtained by “smashing together” the vectors is a matrix. Think of this matrix as your cereal boxes all lined up next to one another on a shelf.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.1. Matrix.

    If \(S = \{\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\subset \R^m\text{,}\) then the associated matrix is \(A_S= [\vect{v}_1 | \cdots | \vect{v}_n]\text{.}\) The vectors of \(S\) are the columns of \(A_S\) and we say \(\vect{v}_j\) is the \(j^{th}\) column of \(A_S\text{.}\) When \(S\) is clear from context, we may just write \(A\text{.}\) The symbol \(A\) is the name of the matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    The plural of matrix is matrices.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.2.

    If \(S= \left\{\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}1\\5\end{bmatrix}\right\}\subset \R^2\text{,}\) then the associated matrix is \(A_S = \left[\begin{array}{c|c} 3\amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 5\end{array}\right]\text{.}\) The vector \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\) is the first column of \(A_S\) and the vector \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\5\end{bmatrix}\) is the second column of \(A_S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    You can think of this as a sequence in two directions. (i.e., \(A\in \Mat_{m\times n}(\R)\))
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.3. Vertical bars.

    The vertical bars between column vectors help us visually organize a matrix, but do not serve a computational purpose. I will use them going forward only when they improve the clarity of exposition.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.4. Representing a matrix: name, column form, and entry form.

    We currently have three distinct ways to represent a matrix. The most succinct way to denote a matrix is with its name, say
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A. \end{equation*}
    If we want to unpack it into its column vectors, we can represent it as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} [\vect{v}_1|\cdots | \vect{v}_n]. \end{equation*}
    Furthermore, each of the columns is itself a list of numbers, which we can unpack to obtain a grid of numbers
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{ \left[\begin{array}{c|c|c|c} a_{11}\amp a_{12}\amp\cdots\amp a_{1n}\\ a_{21}\amp a_{22}\amp\cdots\amp a_{2n}\\ \vdots\amp\vdots\amp\ddots\amp \vdots\\ a_{m1}\amp a_{m2}\amp\cdots\amp a_{mn}\\ \end{array}\right]}_{n\ columns} \left.\begin{aligned}{}\\{}\\ \hspace{0pc}\end{aligned}\right\}{\scriptstyle m\ rows}. \end{equation*}
    The symbol \(a_{ij}\) denotes the entry in the \(i^{th}\) row and \(j^{th}\) column. The matrix is all of these: its name, its column form, and its entry form.
    🔗
    🔗
    I will always use capital letters for matrix names, e.g. \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\text{,}\) \(P\text{,}\) etc. I try to always denote entries of a matrix with the associated lower case letters, e.g. \(A=[a_{ij}]\text{,}\) \(B=[b_{ij}]\text{,}\) etc.
    🔗
    How do we decide which representation to use? We will move between these constantly, choosing the representation that best meets our needs at the moment. While the entry form is often best for arithmetic computations, the name and column forms are usually better for us to organize our thoughts and communicate our reasoning.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.5. Matrix Multiplication (Matrix with Vector).

    If \(\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\in\R^m\text{,}\) \(A=[\vect{v}_1|\cdots | \vect{v}_n]\) and \(\vect{x}\in\R^n\text{,}\) then matrix multiplication of \(A\) and \(\vect{x}\) is the linear combination of the columns of \(A\) with corresponding scalars \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\text{.}\) In symbols, \(A\vect{x} = x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +x_n\vect{v}_n.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    At the level of entries, Definition \ref{def_matrixmult} looks like:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\\a_{21}\\ \vdots \\ a_{m1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\vect{v}_1} + x_2\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}a_{12}\\a_{22}\\ \vdots \\ a_{m2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\vect{v}_2} +\cdots+ x_n \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}a_{1n}\\a_{2n}\\ \vdots \\ a_{mn}\end{bmatrix}}_{\vect{v}_n} = \underbrace{ \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}\amp a_{12}\amp\cdots\amp a_{1n}\\ a_{21}\amp a_{22}\amp\cdots\amp a_{2n}\\ \vdots\amp\vdots\amp\ddots\amp \vdots\\ a_{m1}\amp a_{m2}\amp\cdots\amp a_{mn}\\ \end{bmatrix} }_{A} \underbrace{ \begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\x_{2}\\ \vdots \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix} }_{\vect{x}}. \end{equation*}

    Insight 3.1.6.

    Matrix Multiplication Meaning #1: \(A\vect{x}=\vect{b}\) as Mixture: The matrix equation \(A\vect{x}=\vect{b}\) means \(\vect{b}\) is the mixture of the columns of \(A\) according to the serving sizes specified in \(\vect{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.7.

    Example \ref{ex:mixture_parallelogram} could have been written in this language as \(\left[\begin{array}{r|r}2\amp-1\\1\amp1\end{array}\right]\begin{bmatrix}2\\1.5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2.5\\3.5\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 3.1.11. Compactifying Algebraic Properties of Linear Combination.

    Recall Proposition \ref{prop:dist_across_comb}, our “double cookie recipe” proposition, states: If \(x_1,\ldots, x_n, k\in \R\) and \(\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\in\R^m\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} k(x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots+x_n\vect{v}_n) = (kx_1)\vect{v}_1+\cdots+(kx_n)\vect{v}_n. \end{equation*}
    Compactify this algebraic property using matrix multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    For the moment, there is no new concept here, just compact notation. Accordingly, matrix multiplication acquires all the meanings and algebraic properties we have previously established for linear combination.

    Activity 3.1.12. Compactifying Algebraic Properties of Linear Combination.

    Recall Proposition Proposition 2.2.2, our "double cookie recipe" proposition, states:
    🔗
    If \(x_1,\ldots, x_n, k\in \R\) and \(\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\in\R^m\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} k(x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots+x_n\vect{v}_n) = (kx_1)\vect{v}_1+\cdots+(kx_n)\vect{v}_n. \end{equation*}
    Compactify this algebraic property using matrix multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    Matrix multiplication satisfies two fundamental algebraic properties that we will constantly use.
    🔗

    Proposition 3.1.13. Linearity Properties of Matrix Multiplication..

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, \(\vect{x},\vect{y}\in\R^n\text{,}\) and \(k\in \R\text{,}\) then
    1. (Distributes across vector sum) \(A(\vect{x}+\vect{y})=A\vect{x}+A\vect{y},\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. (Commutes with scaling) \(A(k\vect{x})=k(A\vect{x}).\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    These can be verified algebraically from the definition of matrix multiplication (Definition 3.1.5), basic algebraic properties of real numbers (Definition 2.2.6), and basic algebraic properties of linear combination (Proposition 2.2.7).
    🔗
    Here I will give the careful justification of distributivity, with the symbolic movements on the left, and the justification for those symbolic movements on the right. If \(A=[\vect{v}_1|\cdots|\vect{v}_n]\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A(\vect{x}+\vect{y}) \amp= [\vect{v}_1|\cdots|\vect{v}_n] \left( \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}\right) \amp \mbox{Unpacking symbols}\\ \amp= [\vect{v}_1|\cdots|\vect{v}_n] \begin{bmatrix}x_1+y_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n+y_n\end{bmatrix} \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{Def-vector_addition} - vector addition}\\ \amp= (x_1+y_1)\vect{v}_1 + \cdots + (x_n+y_n)\vect{v}_n \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{def_matrixmult} - matrix multiplication}\\ \amp= (x_1\vect{v}_1 + y_1\vect{v}_1)+\cdots+(x_n\vect{v}_n + y_n\vect{v}_n) \amp \mbox{Proposition \ref{prop-fundamental_vector_props} - (LC7)}\\ \amp= (x_1\vect{v}_1 + \cdots + x_n\vect{v}_n)+(y_1\vect{v}_1 + \cdots + y_n\vect{v}_n) \amp \mbox{Proposition \ref{prop-fundamental_vector_props} - (LC1) \& (LC4)}\\ \amp= \left([\vect{v}_1|\cdots|\vect{v}_n] \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ x_n\end{bmatrix}\right) + \left([\vect{v}_1|\cdots|\vect{v}_n] \begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}\right) \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{def_matrixmult} - matrix multiplication}\\ \amp= A\vect{x}+A\vect{y} \amp \mbox{Repacking symbols}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Matrix multiplication of a matrix with another matrix is simply a convenient way of organizing multiple mixtures all at once.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.14. Matrix Multiplication (Matrix with Matrix).

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix and \(B=[\vect{w}_1| \cdots | \vect{w}_p]\) is an \(n\times p\) matrix (i.e. \(\vect{w}_1,\ldots, \vect{w}_p\in\R^n\)), then the matrix multiplication of matrices \(A\) and \(B\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} AB = [\ A\vect{w}_1 \ | \cdots \ | \ A \vect{w}_p\ ].\tag{3.1.1} \end{equation}
    The resulting \(m\times p\) matrix is their product.
    🔗
    🔗
    Columns of a Product. The columns of a product of two matrices are linear combinations of the columns of the first matrix, as read from left to right.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.15.

    \begin{align*} \textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1 \amp -1\\1 \amp 1\end{array}\right]} \textcolor{blue}{\left[\begin{array}{r|r}3 \amp 4\\2 \amp 1\end{array}\right]} \amp= \left[\textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1 \amp -1\\1 \amp 1\end{array}\right]}\textcolor{blue}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}3\\2\end{array}\right]} \ \bigg| \ \textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1 \amp -1\\1 \amp 1\end{array}\right]}\textcolor{blue}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}4\\1\end{array}\right]} \right]\\ \amp= \left[ \textcolor{blue}{3}\textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{r}1\\1\end{array}\right]}+ \textcolor{blue}{2}\textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{r}-1\\1\end{array}\right]} \ \bigg| \ \textcolor{blue}{4}\textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{r}1\\1\end{array}\right]}+ \textcolor{blue}{1}\textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{r}-1\\1\end{array}\right]} \right]\\ \amp= \left[\begin{array}{r|r} (\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{3})+(\textcolor{red}{-1})(\textcolor{blue}{2}) \amp (\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{4})+(\textcolor{red}{-1})(\textcolor{blue}{1})\\ (\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{3})+(\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{2}) \amp (\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{4})+(\textcolor{red}{1})(\textcolor{blue}{1})\end{array}\right]\\ \amp= \left[\begin{array}{r|r}1 \amp 3\\5 \amp 5\end{array}\right] \end{align*}
    🔗

    Proposition 3.1.17. Associativity of Matrix Multiplication..

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, \(B\) is an \(n\times p\) matrix, and \(\vect{x}\in\R^p\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A(B\vect{x})=(AB)\vect{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.

    If \(B=[\vect{w}_1| \cdots | \vect{w}_p]\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A(B\vect{x}) \amp= A([\vect{w}_1|\cdots|\vect{w}_p]\vect{x}) \amp \mbox{Unpack } B\\ \amp= A(x_1\vect{w}_1+\cdots+x_p\vect{w}_p) \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{def_matrixmult} - matrix multiplication}\\ \amp= x_1(A\vect{w}_1)+\cdots+x_p(A\vect{w}_p) \amp \mbox{Proposition \ref{prop_fundamental_matrix_mult_props} - linearity properties}\\ \amp= [A\vect{w}_1|\cdots|A\vect{w}_p]\vect{x} \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{def_matrixmult} - matrix multiplication}\\ \amp= (AB)\vect{x}. \amp \mbox{Definition \ref{Def-matrix_mult_matrices} - matrix multiplication} \end{align*}
