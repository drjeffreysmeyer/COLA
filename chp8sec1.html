    Lesson 7.1 Similar Matrix Decompositions (SMDs)

    In earlier lessons, we have encountered simple matrices, such the standard geometric transformations and elementary matrices, which are easy to understand and work with. However, in general, a matrix looks and acts in complicated ways. A big idea the permeates through mathematics is take a complicated thing and split it into simple pieces that are easy to understand. This is a powerful problem-solving strategy.
    🔗

    Definition 7.1.1. Matrix Decomposition.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix. A matrix decomposition of \(A\) is an expression \(A=B_1B_2\cdots B_r\) where each \(B_i\) is simple in a certain context. When viewed as a matrix transformation, this translates to identifying \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\) as a composition of simple transformations.
    🔗
    🔗
    There are different types of useful matrix decompositions. Associated to each decomposition there are two major problems:
    🔗
    1. Given a decomposition, what kinds of problems can be solved with it?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. How does one find a decomposition, if it exists at all?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In this chapter, we will analyze the following:
    🔗
    1. Similar matrix decomposition (SMD),
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Elementary matrix decomposition, and
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(LU\)-decomposition.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Later in this text, we will cover other important decompositions:
    🔗
    1. \(QR\)-decomposition,
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Polar decomposition, and
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 7.1.2. Similar Matrix Decomposition.

    Given a square matrix \(A\text{,}\) a similar matrix decomposition (SMD) of \(A\) is an expression \(A=PBP^{-1}\text{.}\) We say the two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are similar.
    🔗
    🔗
    While it is not necessary, it is typically our goal that \(B\) here be very simple, such as a stretch, shear, or rotation matrix.
    🔗
    Given a similar matrix decomposition, write \(P=[\vect{v}_1|\cdots |\vect{v}_n]\) and let \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots ,\vect{v}_n\}\text{.}\) We should think of \(P\) as the change of basis matrix from \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates to standard coordinates, and as such, \(P^{-1}\) as the change of basis from standard coordinates to \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates.
    🔗

    Activity 7.1.3. Interpreting Similar Matrix Decomposition.

    In complete sentences, and using geometric words, explain the meaning of each of the following expressions.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp -1\\1\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\amp -1\\1\amp 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle B=\begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle C=\begin{bmatrix}2\amp -1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\0\amp 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\amp -1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle D=\begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\amp -1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    So such a similar matrix decomposition \(A=PBP^{-1}\text{,}\) multiplication by \(A\) can understood as the composition of the following three processes:
    🔗
    Step 1 - \(P^{-1}\)
    🔗
    Change the coordinate of your point from standard to \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates.
    🔗
    Step 2 - \(B\)
    🔗
    Perform the \(B\) transformation in \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates.
    🔗
    Step 3 - \(P\)
    🔗
    Change the coordinate of your point back from \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates to standard coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Technology 7.1.4.

    Play with SMDs and transformations of the plane relative to the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate grid at GeoGebra ID: qahp88bh. Only go to this link after you have done the above activity.
    🔗
    🔗
    Such decompositions are incredibly important in both pure and applied linear algebra. It enables one to take a very complicated looking matrix, and by changing coordinates, understand it as a simple matrix.
    🔗
    You should be able to understand, interpret, and go between symbolic, verbal, and visual representations of such a decomposition, as shown in the following example.
    🔗

    Example 7.1.6.

    Consider the matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2.5\amp -0.5\\-0.5\amp 2.5\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) This matrix does not fall into one of our categories of simple matrices. Looking at what it does to the standard unit square in the plane does not clarify matters much. Below is depicted how it transforms the standard unit square.
    🔗
    Now suppose that we know \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2.5\amp -0.5\\-0.5\amp 2.5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp -1\\1\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\amp -1\\1\amp 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\text{.}\) What this says is we should not be looking at the standard coordinate system, but rather the coordinate system associated to our familiar ordered basis \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1, \vect{v}_2\}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}\text{.}\) With respect to this basis, \(A\) is a stretch by a factor of 2 in the \(\vect{v}_1\)-direction and a stretch by a factor of 3 in the \(\vect{v}_2\)-direction.
    🔗
    🔗
