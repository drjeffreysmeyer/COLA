    Lesson 10.6 Orthogonal Projection

    In classical Euclidean geometry, given a line and a point off of the line, we can drop the perpendicular from the point to the line. I now rephrase this in the language of linear algebra.
    🔗
    Given vectors \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in\R^m\text{,}\) we can orthogonally project \(\vect{w}\) onto the line \(\spn(\vect{v})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We may use some trigonometry and the Angle Equation (10.2.3) to find the length of this projection.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Projection Length } \amp = \norm{\vect{w}}\cos(\theta) \\ \amp = \frac{1}{\norm{\vect{v}}}\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}. \end{align*}
    This projection vector can now be computed.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Projection Vector } \amp = \text{Length} \times \text{Unit Direction}\\ \amp =\left(\frac{1}{\norm{\vect{v}}}\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\norm{\vect{v}}}\vect{v}\right)\\ \amp = \frac{\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}}{\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{v}}}\vect{v} \end{align*}

    Definition 10.6.1. Projection Component.

    The projection component of \(\vect{w}\) onto \(\vect{v}\) is the vector:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{proj}_{\vect{v}}(\vect{w}) %= ||\vect{w}|| \cos(\theta) \ \widehat{\vect{v}} %= \ip{\vect{w}}{\widehat{\vect{v}}} \widehat{\vect{v}} = \frac{\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{w}}}{\ip{\vect{v}}{\vect{v}}}\vect{v}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Observe that \(\mathrm{proj}_{\vect{v}}(\vect{w}) \in \spn(\vect{v})\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 10.6.2. Orthogonal Component.

    The orthogonal component of \(\vect{w}\) relative to \(\vect{v}\) is the vector:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{orth}_{\vect{v}}(\vect{w})= \vect{w} - \mathrm{proj}_{\vect{v}}(\vect{w}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Observe that \(\mathrm{orth}_{\vect{v}}(\vect{w}) \in \spn(\vect{v})^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Viewing \(\vect{w}\) as the hypotenuse of a right triangle, its legs become the projection component and orthogonal component. Every vector has such a decomposition.
    🔗

    Instructions.

    An interactive GeoGebra window showing projection components and orthogonal components. Move the vector \(\vect{w}\) around and observe how the projection component and orthogonal component change.
    🔗
    🔗
    Play with projections at GeoGebra ID: vgtq44gs.
    🔗
    These ideas are at are at the core of the results in the next couple sections and are also incredible valuable in understanding vector calculus.
    🔗
    Suppose now that \(S\subset \R^m\) is independent. We may generalize the ideas of orthogonal projection from lines to considering orthogonal projections onto \(W = \spn(S)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Given any \(\vect{b}\in \R^m\text{,}\) we can drop the perpendicular to a point on \(W\text{.}\) This gives us a decomposition of \(\vect{b}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{b} = \mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})+\mathrm{orth}_W(\vect{b}), \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \(\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})\in W\) is its projection component
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \(\mathrm{orth}_W(\vect{b})\in W^\perp\) is its orthogonal component.
    🔗
    How do we actually find \(\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Since \(\vect{b} = \mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})+\mathrm{orth}_W(\vect{b})\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A^T \vect{b} \amp =A^T(\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b}))+A^T(\mathrm{orth}_W(\vect{b}))\\ \amp = A^T(\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})) \\ \amp = (A^TA)[\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})]_S. \end{align*}

    Proposition 10.6.3. Independent \(S\) Implies Invertible Gram Matrix.

    If \(S\) is independent, then \(A^TA\) is invertible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    A square matrix is invertible if and only if its null space is trivial. Pick \(\vect{x}\in \Null(A^TA)\text{.}\) I will show that it must be zero.
    🔗
    By definition of null space, \((A^TA)\vect{x}=\vect{0}\text{.}\) By associativity of matrix multiplication, \(0 = (A^TA)\vect{x}=A^T(A\vect{x})\text{,}\) and by Theorem 10.5.7, \(A\vect{x}\in \spn(S)^\perp\text{.}\) Meanwhile, the definition of matrix multiplication implies \(A\vect{x}\in \spn(S)\text{.}\) That means \(\norm{A\vect{x}}^2=\ip{A\vect{x}}{A\vect{x}}=0\text{,}\) and hence \(A\vect{x}=0\text{.}\) So \(A\vect{x}=\vect{0}\) and the positive definiteness of the inner product Proposition 10.2.4 implies \(\vect{x}\in \Null(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By assumption, \(S\) is independent, and therefore, by Proposition 3.2.3, \(\Null(A)=\{\vect{0}\}\) and thus it must be the case that \(\vect{x}=\vect{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Since the Gram matrix is invertible, we may multiply by its inverse to solve for the \(S\)-coordinates of the projection component of \(\vect{b}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} [\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})]_S =(A^TA)^{-1} A^T \vect{b}, \end{equation*}
    and finally, we change back to standard coordinates,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b}) = A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T \vect{b}. \end{equation*}

    Definition 10.6.4. Orthogonal Projection Matrix.

    If \(A\) is a matrix with independent columns, then \(A (A^TA)^{-1} A^T\) is the orthogonal projection matrix onto \(\Col(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Multiplying by this matrix has the effect of dropping the perpendicular!!!
    🔗
    Let \(A=[\vect{v}_1| \cdots|\vect{v}_n]\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix with independent columns, \(W = \mathrm{Col}(A)\text{,}\) \(S=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots,\vect{v}_n\}\text{,}\) and \(\vect{b} \in \R^m\text{,}\)
    🔗
    If the columns of \(A\) do not span \(\R^n\text{,}\) the matrix equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vect{x} = \vect{b} \hspace{2pc} \text{generically has no solution.} \end{equation*}
    but we can find "the closest possible solution," namely the orthogonal projection onto the column space, i.e. the matrix equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vect{x} = \mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b}) \hspace{2pc} \text{always has a solution.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(\mathrm{proj}_W(\vect{b})\) is the point on \(W\) that is closest to \(\vect{b}\text{,}\) the solution to this matrix equation is the best possible approximate solution to the original matrix equation. Since distance is given by a sum of squares, this solution minimizes the sum of squares. As such we call this solution the linear least squares (LLS) solution.
    🔗

    Definition 10.6.5. Linear Least Squares (LLS) Solution.

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix with independent columns, \(S\text{,}\) and \(\vect{b}\in \R^m\text{,}\) then the linear least squares solution to the matrix equation \(A\vect{x}=\vect{b}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{x}_{LLS} = [\mathrm{proj}_{\Col(A)}(\vect{b})]_S =(A^TA)^{-1} A^T \vect{b}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Unfortunately in the wild, the LLS solution is often denoted with a hat, \(\widehat{\vect{x}}\text{,}\) however this could be confused with normalized vectors. To avoid confusion, I will denote it denoted \(\vect{x}_{LLS}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    LLS solutions have many important applications. For example, in statistics, when we have data sets and want to find a predictive curve (e.g. line) of ``best fit.’’
    🔗

    Example 10.6.6.

    We measure a quantity we believe varies linearly over time. We measure: \(y=2.1\) at \(x=0\text{,}\) \(y=3.5\) at \(x=1\text{,}\) and \(y=3.9\) at \(x=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We want to find the best possible \(f(x) = a_0+a_1x\text{.}\) There is noise, so it will not be a perfect fit.
    🔗
    If the data was perfectly linear, it would satisfy:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} f(0) = a_0 1 + a_10\\ f(1) = a_0 1 + a_11\\ f(2) = a_0 1 + a_12 \end{gather*}
    This amounts to solving the following matrix equation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\ 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 2\\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} 2.1\\ 3.5\\ 3.9\\ \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The data is not perfectly linear, and correspondingly, there is no solution to this matrix equation, but there is a LLS solution!!!
    🔗
    Check for yourself that \(f(x)\approx 2.27+0.9x\) is an excellent fit for this data.
    🔗
    🔗
    Having gone through this example, let’s walk through the general case. out in the wild, there are two covarying quantities, \(x\) an independent quantity and \(y\) a dependent quantity. You suspect that the underlying relationship between \(x\) and \(y\) can be modeled as a linear combination of linearly independent functions (e.g. monomials, trigonometric functions, exponentials, etc.) \(y= f(x) = a_0 f_1(x) + \cdots a_rf_r(x)\text{.}\) To make this concrete, I will explicitly walk through linear relationships. So you suspect \(f(x) = a_01 + a_1x\) for some unknown \(a_0\) and \(a_1\text{.}\) You go out and make \(m\) measurements at regular intervals of \(x\text{,}\) let’s say at times \(x=0,\ldots, m-1\text{.}\) Let \(\vect{y}\) denote the column vector of data (a.k.a. observables).
    🔗
    If the data was perfectly linear, it would satisfy:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f(0) \amp = a_0 1 + a_10\\ f(1) \amp = a_0 1 + a_11\\ f(2) \amp = a_0 1 + a_12\\ \vdots\hspace{1pc}\amp \hspace{1pc}\vdots\\ f(m-1) \amp = a_0 1 + a_1(m-1) \end{align*}
    This amounts to solving the following matrix equation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\ 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 2\\ \vdots\amp \vdots\\ 1\amp m-1\\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{bmatrix} = \vect{y} \end{equation*}
    The data is not perfectly linear, and correspondingly, there is no solution to this matrix equation, but there is a LLS solution. The LLS solution for \(\vect{a}\) gives line of best fit for the data you observe!!!
    🔗
