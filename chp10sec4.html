    Lesson 9.4 Classification in the Plane

    In this lesson, our goal is to completely understand the situation for \(2\times 2 \) matrices. As we shall see, our analysis boils down to whether we can find 2, 1, or 0 linearly independent eigenvectors. To understand eigenvectors, we first understand eigenvalues, and to understand eigenvalues, we first study the characteristic polynomial.
    🔗
    I now introduce/remind you of a few important terms.
    🔗

    Definition 9.4.1. \(2\times 2\) Eigentheory Dictionary.

    If \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a \amp b\\c \amp d\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then:
    🔗
    1. The determinant of \(A\) is \(\det(A)=ad-bc\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. The trace of \(A\) is \(\tr(A)=a+d\text{,}\) i.e. the sum of the diagonal entries.
      🔗
    3. The discriminant of \(A\) is \(\disc(A)=\tr(A)^2-4\det(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    4. The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is \(p_A(\lambda)=\det\left[ \begin{array}{ll}a-\lambda \amp b\\c \amp d-\lambda \end{array}\right]\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    It is a straight forward activity to verify the following computational shortcuts.
    🔗

    Proposition 9.4.2. \(2\times 2\) Characteristic Polynomial Formula.

    If \(A\) is \(2\times 2\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\tr(A)\lambda+\det(A). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.

    Proof Hint. Homework. You might find Definition 9.4.1 and Theorem 8.3.3 useful.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 9.4.3.

    If \(A\) is a \(2\times 2\) matrix and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{$\disc(A)$} \begin{cases} > 0, \amp \text{ then $A$ has 2 distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$,}\\ =0, \amp \text{ then $A$ has 1 repeated real eigenvalue $\lambda$,}\\ < 0, \amp \text{ then $A$ has 2 complex conjugate eigenvalues $\lambda=\mu\pm\nu i$.} \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.

    Applying the quadratic formula to the \(2\times 2\) Characteristic Polynomial Formula Proposition 9.4.2 gives that the roots of \(p_A(\lambda)\) are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\tr(A)\pm\sqrt{\tr(A)^2-4\det(A)}}{2}\hspace{1pc}=\hspace{1pc} \frac{\tr(A)\pm\sqrt{\disc(A)}}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We now are prepared to consider the three possible cases.
    🔗
    Case 1: Eigenbasis and Diagonalization.
    🔗
    Suppose \(A\) has two linearly independent (real) eigenvectors \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2\}\text{,}\) corresponding to eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\text{.}\) This is the best of all possible situations and is the simplest to deal with. This occurs when either \(\disc(A)>0\) or when \(\disc(A)=0\) and \(A\) is a scalar matrix (in which case \(A\) uniformly stretches the plane by a factor of \(\lambda=\lambda_1=\lambda_2\)). In this case, we say \(\mathcal{B}\) is an eigenbasis for \(A\) and \(A\) admits a diagonalization \(A=PDP^{-1}\) where \(P=[\vect{v}_1|\vect{v}_2]\) and \(D=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 \amp 0\\0 \amp \lambda_2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Insight 9.4.4. Eigenbasis Summary.

    • Discriminant: \(\disc(A)>0\) or \(\disc(A)=0\) and \(A\) is a scalar matrix,
      🔗
    • Eigenvalues: 2 distinct real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\text{,}\) or \(A\) is scalar.
      🔗
    • Basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{:}\) Two linearly independent eigenvectors \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Similar Matrix Decomp: Diagonalization \(A=[\vect{v}_1|\vect{v}_2]\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 \amp 0\\0 \amp \lambda_2\end{bmatrix}[\vect{v}_1|\vect{v}_2]^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Verbalization: \(A\) transforms the plane by stretching (and possibly reflecting if negative) by a factor of \(\lambda_1\) in the direction of \(\vect{v}_1\) and stretching (and possibly reflecting if negative) by a factor of \(\lambda_2\) in the direction of \(\vect{v}_2\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Case 2: Generalized Eigenbasis and Triagonalization.
    🔗
    Next, suppose \(A\) has only one linearly independent eigenvector \(\vect{v}\) with repeated real eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\) This occurs when \(\disc(A)=0\) and \(A\) is not a scalar matrix. In this case, we do not have enough vectors to form an eigenbasis, but we can find a nice vector to toss in to create a basis. Morally \(A\) shears along its eigenspace, so our idea is to find the vector \(\vect{w}\) we hold onto when we shear. This vector \(\vect{w}\text{,}\) which we call a generalized eigenvector, should satisfy
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vect{w}=\lambda \vect{w} + \vect{v} \end{equation*}
    Observe that this amounts to solving the linear system \((A-\lambda I)\vect{w}=\vect{v}\text{.}\) We call \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v},\vect{w}\}\) be the generalized ordered eigenbasis. In this case, \(A\) admits a triagonalization \(A=PTP^{-1}\) where \(P=[\vect{v}|\vect{w}]\) and \(T=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda \amp 1\\0 \amp \lambda\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Verbally, relative to its generalized eigenbasis, \(A\) is a uniform stretch by a factor of \(\lambda\text{,}\) follows by shearing the vector \(\lambda \vect{w}\) one unit in the \(\vect{v}\)-direction.
    🔗
    You can play with triagonalizations at GeoGebra ID: \href{https://www.geogebra.org/m/j8jvewtk}{j8jvewtk}.
    🔗

    Insight 9.4.5. Generalized Eigenbasis Summary.

    • Discriminant: \(\disc(A)=0\) and \(A\) is not a scalar matrix,
      🔗
    • Eigenvalues: 1 repeated real eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{:}\) Eigenvector \(\vect{v}\) and a generalized eigenvector \(\vect{w}\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Similar Matrix Decomp: Triagonalization \(A=[\vect{v}|\vect{w}]\begin{bmatrix}\lambda \amp 1\\0 \amp \lambda\end{bmatrix}[\vect{v}|\vect{w}]^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Verbalization: \(A\) transforms the plane by uniformly stretching the plane by a factor of \(\lambda\) and then shearing the vector \(\lambda\vect{w}\) one unit in the \(\vect{v}\)-direction.
      🔗
    🔗
    Case 3: Rotation Basis and Rotationalization.
    🔗
    Suppose \(A\) has only no linearly independent real eigenvectors. This occurs when \(\disc(A)\lt 0\text{.}\) Then \(A\) has two complex
     1 
    See Appendix for an overview of complex numbers \(\C\text{.}\)
    eigenvalues: \(\lambda=\mu+\nu i=re^{i\theta}\) and its complex conjugate \(\overline{\lambda}=\mu-\nu i=re^{-i\theta}\text{.}\) Associated to each are complex conjugate eigenvectors \(\vect{v}=\vect{u}+\vect{w}i\) and \(\overline{\vect{v}}=\vect{u}-\vect{w}i\text{,}\) where \(\vect{u}\) and \(\vect{w}\) are real. Since \(\vect{v}\) and \(\overline{\vect{v}}\) are linearly independent (over \(\C\)), it follows that \(\vect{u}\) and \(\vect{w}\) are linearly independent (over \(\R\)), and I call the collection \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{u},\vect{w}\}\) a rotation basis for \(A\text{.}\) (Note that this name is nonstandard.)
    🔗
    How does \(A\) transform the rotation basis? Observe that \(\vect{u}=\frac{1}{2}(\vect{v}+\overline{\vect{v}})\) and \(\vect{w}=\frac{1}{2i}(\vect{v}-\overline{\vect{v}})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A\vect{u} \amp =A\left(\frac{1}{2}(\vect{v}+\overline{\vect{v}})\right) =\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda \vect{v}+\overline{\lambda}\overline{\vect{v}}\right)\\ \amp =\frac{1}{2}\left((\mu+\nu i) (\vect{u}+i\vect{w})+(\mu-\nu i)(\vect{u}-i\vect{w})\right)\\ \amp =\frac{1}{2}\left((\mu\vect{u}+\rst{\mu i\vect{w}}+\rst{\nu i\vect{u}}-\nu \vect{w})+(\mu\vect{u}-\rst{\mu i\vect{w}}-\rst{\nu i\vect{u}}-\nu \vect{w})\right)\\ \amp =\frac{1}{2}\left(2\mu\vect{u}-2\nu \vect{w}\right) = \mu\vect{u}-\nu \vect{w}. \end{align*}
    Similarly \(A\vect{w} = \nu\vect{u}+\mu \vect{w}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence \(A\) admits a rotationalization \(A=PRP^{-1}\) where \(P=[\vect{u}|\vect{w}]\) and \(R\) is the stretch/rotation matrix \(R=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}\mu \amp \nu\\ -\nu \amp \mu \end{array}\right]= \left[ \begin{array}{rr}r\cos(\theta) \amp r\sin(\theta)\\ -r\sin(\theta) \amp r\cos(\theta) \end{array}\right]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    You can play with finding and building rotationalizations, respectively, at GeoGebra IDs: \href{https://www.geogebra.org/m/ua9cd34u}{ua9cd34u} and \href{https://www.geogebra.org/m/tbmrXJmd}{tbmrXJmd}.
    🔗

    Insight 9.4.6. Rotation Basis Summary.

    • Discriminant: \(\disc(A)\lt 0\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Eigenvalues: 2 complex conjugate complex eigenvalues \(\mu\pm\nu i=re^{\pm i\theta}\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{:}\) Rotation vectors \(\vect{u},\vect{w}\) where the complex eigenvectors are \(\vect{u}\pm i\vect{w}\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • Similar Matrix Decomp: Rotationalization \(A=[\vect{u}|\vect{w}]\left[ \begin{array}{rr}r\cos(\theta) \amp r\sin(\theta)\\-r\sin(\theta) \amp r\cos(\theta) \end{array}\right][\vect{u}|\vect{w}]^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Verbalization: \(A\) is a uniform stretch by a factor of \(r=\sqrt{\mu^2+\nu^2}\text{,}\) followed by a rotation in \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates by \(\theta=\arccos\frac{\mu}{r}\) degree sending \(\vect{u}\) away from \(\vect{w}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.4.7.

    Consider the matrix \(A=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}1.5 \amp 1\\-0.25 \amp 2.5 \end{array}\right].\) A direct computation shows
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \disc(A)=4^2-4\cdot 4=0, \end{equation*}
    hence \(A\) has 1 repeated real eigenvalue, and since \(A\) is not scalar, we will look to find a triagonalization for \(A\text{.}\) It has characteristic polynomial
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-4\lambda+4=(\lambda-2)^2, \end{equation*}
    so its eigenvalue is \(\lambda=2\text{.}\) Plugging \(\lambda\) into the standard matrix equation and row reducing, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{rr|r}-0.5 \amp 1 \amp 0\\ -0.25 \amp 0.5 \amp 0 \end{array}\right]\sim\left[ \begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp -2 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right] \end{equation*}
    From which we get that \(x=2y\text{,}\) and hence we get the eigenvector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{v}=\left[ \begin{array}{r}2\\1 \end{array}\right]. \end{equation*}
    We now find a generalized eigenvector that we hold on to to shear after having performed our uniform stretch, i.e. a vector \(\vect{w}\) such that \(A\vect{w}=2\vect{w}+\vect{v}\text{.}\) In other words, we solve the following matrix equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{rr|r}-0.5 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ -0.25 \amp 0.5 \amp 1 \end{array}\right]\sim\left[ \begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp -2 \amp -4\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right] \end{equation*}
    From which we get that \(x=-4+2y\text{,}\) and hence, when \(y=1\text{,}\) we get the generalized eigenvector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{w}=\left[ \begin{array}{r}-2\\1 \end{array}\right]. \end{equation*}
    The vectors \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\) are linearly independent and \(\mathcal{B}:=\{\vect{v},\vect{w}\}\) is our generalized eigenbasis.
    🔗
    Symbolically we have the triagonalization
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{rr}1.5 \amp 1\\-0.25 \amp 2.5 \end{array}\right]=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2 \amp -2\\1 \amp 1 \end{array}\right]\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2 \amp 1\\0 \amp 2 \end{array}\right]\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2 \amp -1\\1 \amp 1 \end{array}\right]^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    Verbally, \(A\) is a uniform stretch by a factor of \(2\) followed by a shear in the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates, taking the vector \(2\vect{w}\) 1 unit in the \(\vect{v}\) direction.
    🔗
    Visually, we have the following picture.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.4.9.

    Consider the matrix \(A=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}3 \amp 2\\-5 \amp 5 \end{array}\right].\) A direct computation shows
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \disc(A)=8^2-4\cdot 25=-36\lt 0, \end{equation*}
    hence \(A\) has 2 complex eigenvalues and we will look to find a rotationalization for \(A\text{.}\) It has characteristic polynomial
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-8\lambda+25 \end{equation*}
    and the quadratic formula gives its eigenvalues to be \(4\pm 3i\text{.}\) We focus on the eigenvalue \(\lambda=4+ 3i=5e^{\arccos(4/5)i}\text{.}\) Plugging \(\lambda\) into the matrix equation \((A-\lambda I)\vect{x}=\vect{0}\) and row reducing, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ll|r}-1-3i \amp 2 \amp 0\\ -5 \amp 1-3i \amp 0 \end{array}\right]\sim\left[ \begin{array}{lr|r}1+3i \amp -2 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right] \end{equation*}
    From which we get that \(y=\frac{(1+3i)}{2}x\text{,}\) and hence we get the complex eigenvector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{v}=\left[ \begin{array}{ll}2\\1+3i \end{array}\right]=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2\\1 \end{array}\right]+\left[ \begin{array}{rr}0\\3 \end{array}\right]i. \end{equation*}
    Here \(\vect{u}:=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2\\1 \end{array}\right]\) is the real part and \(\vect{w}:=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}0\\3 \end{array}\right]\) is the imaginary part of the eigenvector. They are linearly independent and \(\mathcal{B}:=\{\vect{u},\vect{w}\}\) is our rotation basis.
    🔗
    Direct computation using the strategy above gives \(A\vect{u} = 4\vect{u}-3 \vect{w}\) and \(A\vect{w} = 3\vect{u}+4 \vect{w}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Symbolically we have the rotationalization
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{rr}3 \amp 2\\-5 \amp 5 \end{array}\right]=\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2 \amp 0\\1 \amp 3 \end{array}\right]\left[ \begin{array}{rr}4 \amp 3\\-3 \amp 4 \end{array}\right]\left[ \begin{array}{rr}2 \amp 0\\1 \amp 3 \end{array}\right]^{-1}. \end{equation*}
    Verbally, \(A\) is a uniform stretch by a factor of \(r=5\) followed by a rotation in \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates by an angle of \(\theta =\arccos\left(\frac{4}{5}\right)\) radians moving \(\vect{u}\) away from \(\vect{w}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Visually, we have the following picture. Note that the \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit circle is dashed red, and the transformed \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit circle is dashed blue. Note how the vertices \((8,-5)\) and \((6,15)\) of the transformed \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square lie on this ellipse.
