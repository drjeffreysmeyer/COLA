    Lesson 3.3 Matrix Multiplication as Transformation

    Subsection Transforming Points and Objects

    Definition 3.3.1. Matrix Transformation.

    If \(A=[\vect{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \vect{v}_n]\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, then the rule where you input the serving size vector \(\vect{x}\in \mathbb{R}^n\) and output the mixture vector \(A\vect{x}= x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +x_n\vect{v}_n\in \mathbb{R}^m\) is a matrix transformation from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we give the transformation the name \(T_A\text{,}\) then we write \(T_A:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) such that \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\text{,}\) or equivalently, \(T_A(\vect{x}):=A\vect{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(T_A(\vect{x})=\vect{b}\text{,}\) then we say \(T_A\) sends \(\vect{x}\) to \(\vect{b}\). When \(A\) is clear from context, we may write \(T\) instead of \(T_A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Insight 3.3.2. Matrix Multiplication Meaning #2: \(A\vect{x}=\vect{b}\) as Transformation.

    The matrix equation \(A\vect{x}=\vect{b}\) means the transformation associated to multiplying by \(A\) sends the input serving size vector \(\vect{x}\) to the output mixture vector \(\vect{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Matrix transformations have very nice geometric properties and model all sorts of phenomena. For example, these transformations and their generalizations are the basis for tools in computer graphics, cryptography, and machine learning.
    🔗

    Example 3.3.3. Transforming a Point.

    Consider the matrix transformation \(T_A:\mathbb{R}^2\to \mathbb{R}^2\) associated to multiplication by \(A=\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For any input vector \(\vect{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(T_A(\vect{x}) = A \vect{x} = x_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + x_2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) So, for example, \(T_A\left(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\right) = (1)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + (2)\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) The transformation \(T_A\) sends vector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) to vector \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let’s visualize this transformation thinking of vectors as points. Observe how on the same plot, we draw both input and output. This allows us to see how \(T_A\) moves the points in the plane.
    🔗
    
    Visualizing the input (serving) vector and the output (mixture) vector on the same plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.4. Notation: \(T\) vs \(A\).

    Please take note of the following subtle but important point: \(T\) is the name of a rule of assignment, while \(A\) is the name of a matrix. Think by analogy in calculus when you looked at the function \(f(x)=2x\text{.}\) The function \(f\) is a rule that doubles the input, while \(2\) is just a number. While this rule and this number are related, they are distinct mathematical objects, just as \(T\) and \(A\) are distinct mathematical objects.
    🔗
    🔗
    Points make up objects. When you look at where a matrix transformation sends all the points of an object, you now have a transformed object!
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.5. Transformed Sets.

    If \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a matrix transformation and \(X\subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is a set of points, then the transformed set is the set of points in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) obtained by recording where \(T\) sends each point of \(X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In symbols \(T(X) = \{ T(\vect{x}) \mid \vect{x}\in X\}\) and we say \(T\) sends \(X\) to \(T(X)\text{.}\) We may also say \(T(X)\) is the image of \(X\) under \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We are interested in understanding where matrix transformations send all sorts of geometric objects, including: lines, line segments, squares, and parallelograms.
    🔗

    Example 3.3.6. Transforming a Line.

    Consider the matrix transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to \mathbb{R}^2\) associated to multiplication by \(A=\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and the line \(\ell = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mid t\in \mathbb{R}\right\}\text{.}\) For each \(t\in \mathbb{R}\text{,}\) and hence each point on \(\ell\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} T\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \amp =\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \amp \text{Definition \ref{def-matrix_transformation}}\\ \amp =\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \amp \text{Proposition \ref{prop_fundamental_matrix_mult_props}}\\ \amp =\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \amp \text{Computation} \end{align*}
    🔗
    So \(T\) sends \(\ell\) to the new line \(T(\ell) = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \mid t\in \mathbb{R}\right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Again, let’s visualize this transformation thinking of vectors as points. Lines are composed of points. Each value of parameter \(t\) determines a point on the line. Below, we show with the dashed purple curves where several points on \(\ell\) are sent to. By following where \(T\) sends points, we see that every point on the line \(\ell\) is sent to a point on the line \(T(\ell)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    By following the individual points that comprise the line, we may visualize the line and the associated transformed line on the same plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    Visualize how playing with the entries of a \(2\times 2\) matrix affects how the associated matrix transformation moves lines in the plane at GeoGebra ID: abuf7abt.
    🔗

    Proposition 3.3.7. Matrix transformations send lines to lines.

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^m\) is the associated transformation \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\text{,}\) \(\vect{p},\vect{v}\in \mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) \(J\subset \mathbb{R}\) denotes either a closed interval or all of \(\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) and \(\ell = \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v} \mid t\in J\}\) is a line (segment), then either:
    🔗
    \(A\vect{v}=\vect{0}\text{,}\) in which case \(T(\ell)=A\vect{p}\) (i.e., the whole line is sent to one point), otherwise,
    🔗
    the transformed line (segment) \(T(\ell)\) is also a line (segment) and has parametric vector form \(\{A\vect{p}+tA\vect{v} \mid t\in J\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    If \(t\in J\text{,}\) then \(\vect{p}+t\vect{v}\in \ell\) and:
    \begin{align*} T(\vect{p}+t\vect{v}) \amp =A(\vect{p}+t\vect{v})\\ \amp =(A\vect{p})+t(A\vect{v}) \amp \text{Proposition \ref{prop_fundamental_matrix_mult_props}} \end{align*}
    If \(A\vect{v}=\vect{0}\text{,}\) then \(T(\vect{p}+t\vect{v})=A\vect{p}\text{.}\) Otherwise if \(A\vect{v}\ne\vect{0}\) and \(\ell' = \{A\vect{p}+tA\vect{v} \mid t\in J\}\text{,}\) then we have shown that for each \(t\in J\text{,}\) \(T\) identifies the point on \(\ell\) at time \(t\) with the point on \(\ell'\) at time \(t\text{,}\) and hence \(T(\ell)=\ell'\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 3.3.8. Matrix transformations send \(\vect{0}\) to \(\vect{0}\).

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix and \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^m\) is the transformation \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\text{,}\) then \(T(\vect{0})=\vect{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This justification formalizes the intuition that mixing zero serving sizes of vectors results in the zero mixture. If \(A=[\vect{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \vect{v}_n]\text{,}\) then \(T(\vect{0})=A\vect{0}=[\vect{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \vect{v}_n]\vect{0}=0\vect{v}_1+\cdots+0\vect{v}_n=\vect{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 3.3.9. Matrix transformations send subspaces to subspaces.

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^m\) is the transformation \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\) and \(W\subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is a subspace, then the transformed subspace \(T(W)\subset \mathbb{R}^m\) is a subspace.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This justification is an activity in reasoning about properties. Subspaces are defined by having three properties (Definition Definition 2.6.1). We use the assumption that \(W\) has these properties to deduce \(T(W)\) also has these properties. In this context, you want to show:
    🔗
    1. Contains Zero. \(\vect{0}\in T(W)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Closed Under Addition. If \(T(\vect{u}),T(\vect{w})\in T(W)\text{,}\) then \(T(\vect{u})+T(\vect{w})\in T(W)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Closed Under Scaling. If \(T(\vect{w})\in T(W)\) and \(k\in \mathbb{R}\text{,}\) then \(kT(\vect{w})\in T(W)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The details of this justification are left as an activity. As a hint, Definition 2.6.1, Definition 3.3.5, Proposition 3.3.8, and Proposition 3.1.13 may be useful.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Transformations of the Plane

    For the moment, we will restrict our focus to matrix transformations associated to \(2\times 2\) matrices. Such transformations are transformations of the plane. What do matrix transformations do to the plane? The plane is big (in fact infinite!), so it is useful to restrict our focus to well-chosen small subsets of the plane. Perhaps the most reasonable place to start is a square.
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.10. Geometric Words and Geometric Gestures.

    Imagine a square is made out of elastic and you are holding it in your hands. What some clear geometric words and gestures you could use to instruct someone transform that elastic square?
    🔗
    1. Stretch by a certain factor in a given direction.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Reflect across a given line.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Rotate by a certain number of degrees in a given direction.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Project onto a line (or origin).
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Shear a line (or vector) to another line while holding another line fixed.
      🔗
      🔗
    
    A cartoon depicting transforming the unit square.
    🔗
    🔗
    We usually visualize transformations of the plane by drawing both the input and output on the same plane. This gives us a sense of the movement of the transformation.
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.11. (Standard) Unit Square.

    The (standard) unit square is the parallelogram \(S\) in the Euclidean plane with vertices: \(O=(0,0)\text{,}\) \(P=(1,0)\text{,}\) \(Q=(1,1)\text{,}\) and \(R=(0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    Visualizing the unit square and transformed unit square on the same plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    As a consequence of Proposition 3.3.7 and Proposition 3.3.8, to see what \(T\) does to the unit square \(S\text{,}\) it suffices to follow the three vertices \(P\text{,}\) \(Q\text{,}\) and \(R\text{.}\) Note that it is always important to clearly follow and label each vertex, as otherwise the pictures could mislead you.
    🔗

    Example 3.3.12. Transforming a Square.

    In this example, we walk through how to use matrix multiplication and the unit square \(S\) to reason about how \(T(\vect{x})=\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\vect{x}\) transforms the plane.
    🔗
    By the above remarks, it suffices to follow what \(T\) does to \(P\text{,}\) \(Q\text{,}\) and \(R\text{.}\) This amounts to the following three computations:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} P'\amp=T\left(\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}\\ Q'\amp=T\left(\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}\\ R'\amp=T\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\3\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    We plot these points, and since line segments are sent to line segments, we connect them up. We see we have drawn a rectangle.
    🔗
    
    Visualizing the unit square and transformed unit square on the same plane.
    🔗
    Now we think to ourselves, if the unit square is made of elastic, and I am holding it within my hands, how can I describe what I would have to do to it to obtain the transformed square? We see that we stretch the square by a factor of 2 in the \(x\)-direction and we stretch the square by a factor of 3 in the \(y\)-direction.
    🔗
    We have now created visual and verbal representations of the transformation given by the symbolic expression \(T(\vect{x})=\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\vect{x}\text{.}\)

    Definition 3.3.16. Identity Matrix.

    The \(n\times n\) matrix with 1’s along the diagonal and all other entries \(0\) is the \(n\times n\) identity matrix. We typically name the identity matrix \(I\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 3.3.17.

    If \(I\) is the \(n\times n\) identity matrix and \(\vect{x}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then \(I\vect{x}=\vect{x}\text{.}\)

    While stretches, reflections, projections, and shears relative to the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes are relatively straight forward and easy to identify (once you get the hang of it) rotations are a little more subtle. I will now go over what a rotation matrix looks like.
    🔗
    Draw the unit square and then rotate it by \(\theta\) radians counterclockwise (see Figure 3.3.18). What matrix sends \(S\) to this rotated square?
    🔗
    To figure this out, I think about matrix multiplication as mixture (Matrix Multiplication Meaning #1 Insight 3.1.6) and the unit square \(S\) to reason about the columns of a rotation matrix. Suppose that \(A=[\vect{v}|\vect{w}]\) is our rotation matrix. Using just a little trigonometry (see Figure 3.3.18 above), we see that \(\left[ \begin{array}{}1\\0\end{array}\right]\) is sent to \(\left[ \begin{array}{}\cos(\theta)\\\sin(\theta)\end{array}\right]\) and \(\left[ \begin{array}{}0\\1\end{array}\right]\) is sent to \(\left[ \begin{array}{r}-\sin(\theta)\\\cos(\theta)\end{array}\right]\text{.}\) This gives us two computations:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{}\cos(\theta)\\\sin(\theta)\end{array}\right] = T\left(\left[ \begin{array}{}1\\0\end{array}\right]\right) = [\vect{v}|\vect{w}]\left[ \begin{array}{}1\\0\end{array}\right] = 1\vect{v}+0\vect{w} = \vect{v} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{r}-\sin(\theta)\\\cos(\theta)\end{array}\right] = T\left(\left[ \begin{array}{}0\\1\end{array}\right]\right) = [\vect{v}|\vect{w}]\left[ \begin{array}{}0\\1\end{array}\right] = 0\vect{v}+1\vect{w} = \vect{w} \end{equation*}
    It follows that the matrix that rotates the plane by \(\theta\) radians counterclockwise is \(\left[ \begin{array}{r|r}\cos(\theta)\amp -\sin(\theta)\\\sin(\theta)\amp \cos(\theta)\end{array}\right]\text{.}\)

    Subsection Transformations of \(\R^n\) for \(n>2\)

    If \(A\) is an \(n \times n\) matrix, where \(n>2\text{,}\) then \(T_A\) is a transformation of \(\R^n\text{.}\) All of the transformations we encountered in the plane generalize to this context. Furthermore, there are no fundamentally new transformations.
    🔗
    For example, the diagonal matrix \(A=\left[ \begin{array}{}2\amp 0\amp 0\\0\amp 3\amp 0\\0\amp 0\amp 5\end{array}\right]\) transforms the unit cube by stretching the \(x\)-direction by a factor of 2, stretching the \(y\)-direction by a factor of 3, and stretching the \(z\)-direction by a factor of 5.
