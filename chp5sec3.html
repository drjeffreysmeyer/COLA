    Lesson 5.3 Concepts of Basis

    In the previous two lessons, we explored deeply dependencies and spans. In some sense, these two concepts are complementary, and in this lesson, we bring them together.
    🔗
    To begin, our first deep theorem relates independent vectors and span within subspaces.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.1. Independent Sets Are No Bigger Than Spanning Sets.

    If \(W\subset \vect{R}^m\) is a subspace, \(S = \{\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\subset \vect{R}^m\) such that \(W=\spn(S)\text{,}\) and \(I = \{\vect{w}_1,\ldots, \vect{w}_r\}\subset W\) is an independent collection, then \(r\le n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    In this proof, I outline an iterative process for one-by-one replacing the elements of \(S\) with elements of \(I\) while preserving the span, and in so doing, deduce that \(r\le n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To begin, let \(S_1' = \{\vect{w}_1\}\cup S = \{\vect{w}_1, \vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\text{.}\) By assumption, \(\vect{w}_1\) lies in the span of \(S\text{,}\) hence \(S_1'\) is dependent. It follows from Dependency Analysis Insight 5.1.3 that there exists a nonpivot column in \(A_{S_1'}\text{,}\) and we may toss out the first one we see and maintain the same span. While I do not know which vector we tossed, I know it was not the first, \(\vect{w}_1\text{.}\) Write \(S_1\) for this new collection. Since I included one vector and tossed out a vector, I know \(S_1\) has \(n\) vectors (and \(W = \spn(S)=\spn(S_1)\)).
    🔗
    Now iterate this process \(r\) times: For each \(j\le r\text{,}\) let \(S_j' = \{\vect{w}_j\} \cup S_{j-1}\text{.}\) Since \(\vect{w}_j\in W=\spn(S_{j-1})\text{,}\) the collection \(S_j'\) is dependent. By removing the first nonpivot column of \(A_{S_j'}\text{,}\) we have a collection of \(n\) vectors \(S_j\) with the same span as \(S_{j-1}\text{.}\) While I do not know which vector we tossed, I know it was not any of the first \(j\text{,}\) \(\vect{w}_1, \vect{w}_2,\ldots, \vect{w}_j\text{,}\) as those are all independent, and hence would all be pivot columns in \(A_{S_j'}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We conclude that \(S_r\) is a set of \(n\) vectors whose span is \(W\) and \(I\subset S_r\text{.}\) Hence \(r\le n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 5.3.2.

    If \(W\subset \vect{R}^m\) is a subspace and \(S\) and \(S'\) are both independent collections of vectors that span \(W\text{,}\) then \(S\) and \(S'\) have the same number of vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    This important result means that if I find a collection of \(d\) many independent vectors that spans \(W\text{,}\) then all other independent collections that span \(W\) also have size \(d\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.3. Dimension.

    If \(W\subset \vect{R}^m\) is a subspace, and \(S\subset W\) is an independent collection that spans \(W\text{,}\) then the dimension of \(W\text{,}\) denoted \(\dim W\text{,}\) is the size of \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    You have seen that an independent collection is not too big. You have seen that a spanning collection is not too small. As I shall now explain, a basis is a Goldilocks collection — it is just right.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.4. Basis.

    If \(W\subset \vect{R}^m\) is a subspace and \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots \vect{v}_n\}\subset W\) is a collection of linearly independent vectors that spans \(W\text{,}\) then \(\mathcal{B}\) is a basis for \(W\text{.}\) An ordered basis is a basis where we fix once and for all the order in which we list them.
    🔗
    🔗
    The plural of basis is bases.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.5. Standard Basis of \(\vect{R}^n\).

    The columns of the \(n\times n\) identity matrix, \(\mathcal{E}=\{\vect{e}_1,\ldots, \vect{e}_n\}\text{,}\) is a basis for \(\vect{R}^n\text{,}\) which we call the standard basis. To see this, observe that if \(\vect{v}\in \vect{R}^n\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \vect{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1\\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} = v_1\begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + v_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \cdots + v_n \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = v_1\vect{e}_1+v_2\vect{e}_2+\cdots + v_n\vect{e}_n \end{equation*}
    It also follows that \(\vect{R}^n\) has dimension \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We now come to the Fundamental Theorem of Bases, which guarantees that, if we have a basis, we can make every vector in \(W\) with no redundancy.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.6. Fundamental Theorem of Bases.

    If \(W\subset \vect{R}^m\) is a subspace, \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\subset W\) is an ordered basis for \(W\text{,}\) and \(\vect{w}\in W\) is a vector, then there exists a unique collection of coefficients \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\in \vect{R}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \vect{w}=x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +x_n\vect{v}_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Since \(\mathcal{B}\) spans \(W\text{,}\) \(\vect{w}\in \spn(\mathcal{B})\text{.}\) Suppose now
    \begin{equation*} \vect{w}=x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +x_n\vect{v}_n, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \vect{w}=y_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots +y_n\vect{v}_n, \end{equation*}
    for some \(x_1,\ldots,x_n,y_1,\ldots,y_n\in\vect{R}\text{.}\) Subtracting one from the other gives:
    \begin{equation*} \vect{0}=(x_1-y_1)\vect{v}_1+\cdots +(x_n-y_n)\vect{v}_n. \end{equation*}
    The independence of \(\mathcal{B}\) (Proposition 2.5.6) implies each coefficient \(x_i-y_i=0\text{,}\) or in other words, \(x_i=y_i\text{.}\) We deduce that the coefficients are unique.
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A\) is a matrix, then we know two important associated subspaces: its column space (Definition 3.2.1) and its null space (Definition 3.2.2). You should be able to find their bases.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.7. Basis for Column Space and Null Space.

    Suppose \(A = [\vect{v}_1\mid\vect{v}_2\mid\vect{v}_3]\) and \(\rref\left(A\right) = \begin{bmatrix}1\amp0\amp-2\\0\amp1\amp-3\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) This tells us \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\) are independent, and \(\vect{v}_3\in \spn(\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2)\text{.}\) Hence a basis for \(\Col(A)\) is \(\{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z \end{bmatrix}\in \Null(A)\text{,}\) then:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2z\\3z\\z\end{bmatrix} = z\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    So a basis for \(\Null(A)\) is \(\left\{\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\1\end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 5.3.8.

    You start with an ordered list of five vectors, \(S=\{\vect{v}_1, \ldots \vect{v}_5\}\subset \vect{R}^m\text{.}\) You compute \(\rref(A_S)\text{.}\) For each rref matrix below, find a basis for \(\Col(A_S)\) (in terms of the vectors of \(S\)) and find a basis for \(\Null(A_S)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp0\amp5\amp1\amp2\\ 0\amp1\amp6\amp3\amp1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp4\amp0\amp3\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp1\amp5\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp0\amp0\amp1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp0\amp0\amp0\amp0\\ 0\amp1\amp0\amp0\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp1\amp0\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp0\amp1\amp0\\ 0\amp0\amp0\amp0\amp1\\ 0\amp0\amp0\amp0\amp0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In general, dimension is an excellent way to measure the size of a subspace.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.9. Rank and Nullity.

    If \(A\) is a matrix, then:
    🔗
    1. the dimension of its column space, \(\Col(A)\text{,}\) is the column rank of \(A\text{,}\) written \(\rank(A)\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. the dimension of its null space, \(\Null(A)\text{,}\) is the nullity of \(A\text{,}\) written \(\nullity(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    It follows from Interpretation Insight 5.1.3 that \(\rank(A)\) is equal to the number of columns of \(\rref(A)\) with leading ones. Meanwhile, it follows from Interpretation Insight 4.4.6 that \(\nullity(A)\) is equal to the number of columns of \(\rref(A)\) without leading ones. We deduce the following result.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.10. Rank-Nullity.

    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, then \(\rank(A)+\nullity(A)=n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    I conclude this lesson with a useful computational quick check for determining if a collection of vectors is a basis of \(\vect{R}^m\text{.}\) It follows from Computational Quick Checks Proposition 5.1.6 and Proposition 5.2.7 that, if \(S=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots \vect{v}_n\}\subset \vect{R}^m\text{,}\) then \(S\) is a basis for \(\vect{R}^m\) if and only if:
    🔗
    • \(\rref(A_S)\) has a leading 1 in every column, AND
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\rref(A_S)\) has a leading 1 in every row.
      🔗
      🔗
    If \(\rref(A_S)\) has a leading 1 in every row and every column, \(A_S\) must have the same number of rows as columns, and hence \(m=n\text{.}\) So a basis in \(\vect{R}^m\) must have \(m\) many vectors. Putting these observations together, we get the following quick check.
    🔗

    Algorithm 5.3.11. Computational Quick Check For Basis of \(\vect{R}^m\).

    If \(S=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots \vect{v}_n\}\subset \vect{R}^m\text{,}\) then \(S\) is a basis if and only if \(\rref(A_S)=I\text{,}\) the \(m\times m\) identity matrix.
