    Lesson 8.1 Determinant as Signed Volume Scaling Factor

    Subsection Area

    What is area? More precisely, given a closed polygonal region \(R\text{,}\) what do we mean by the area of \(R\text{?}\) In short, it is the “size” of the region, as measure in units of the unit square \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.1. Area in the Plane.

    If \(R\) is closed polygonal region in the plane, its area, denoted \(\Area(R)\text{,}\) is the number of disjoint copies of the unit square needed to fill \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    🔗
    While completing Activity 8.1.2, it is likely that you discovered and used each of the following properties of area. Take a moment to go back and see all the places you might have used the following properties in the activity.
    🔗

    Proposition 8.1.3. Properties of Area.

    Area has the following three properties:
    1. (Normalization.) The area of the unit square \(S\) is 1 (i.e. \(\Area(S)=1\)),
      🔗
    2. (Translation.) Translation does not affect area (i.e. \(\Area(\vect{v}+R)=\Area (R)\)), and
      🔗
    3. (Additivity.) The area of two disjoint regions is the sum of their area (i.e. if \(R_1\cap R_2=\varnothing\text{,}\) then \(\Area(R_1\cup R_2)=\Area (R_1)+\Area (R_2)\)).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    These three properties will allow us to productively reason about area going forward.
    🔗
    Given a square \(2\times 2\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) it transforms the plane via matrix multiplication \(T:\R^2\to \R^2\text{,}\) \(\vect{x}\mapsto A\vect{x}\text{.}\) We have already seen how we can measure stretch factors in a given direction. A second natural question is the following: How does \(T\) change area?
    🔗
    Some questions to start your investigations:
    🔗
    1. How does the area of a transformed region \(T(R)\text{,}\) and a transformed translated region \(T(\vect{v}+R)\text{,}\) compare?
      🔗
    2. How do each of the elementary geometric matrix transformations affect area?
      🔗
    3. How do compositions affect area?
      🔗
    4. How might matrix decompositions help you better understand how a matrix transforms area?
      🔗
    5. How might we generalize this to higher dimensions?
      🔗
    What else might you wonder about area as it relates to linear algebra?
    🔗

    Example 8.1.6.

    Consider the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 4\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    transforming the red pentagon to the right. The red pentagon has area \(3\text{.}\) The transformed pentagon in blue has area 18.
    🔗
    The ratio of areas is \(\frac{18}{3}=6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    A transformed polygonal region in the plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    Having completed the activity, here are two fundamental observations you should either have made or should now go back and verify.
    🔗
    Observation 1: Changing your polygon does not affect the ratio of areas. Rather, the ratio of areas only depends upon the matrix.
    🔗
    As such, we are justified in making the following definition.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.8. Area Scaling Factor (asf).

    If \(A\) is a \(2\times 2\) matrix and \(R\) is any closed polygonal region, then the area scaling factor of \(A\) is the constant value \(\asf(A) = \frac{\Area(T_A(R))}{\Area(R)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Observation 2: Simple transformations affect area in the following ways.
    🔗
    1. Pure stretches scale area by their stretch factor, \(k\text{.}\) (i.e. \(\asf(A)=k\))
      🔗
    2. Projections smash area to 0. (i.e. \(\asf(A)=0\))
      🔗
    3. Pure reflections do not affect area. (i.e. \(\asf(A)=1\))
      🔗
    4. Pure rotations do not affect area. (i.e. \(\asf(A)=1\))
      🔗
    5. Pure shears do not affect area. (i.e. \(\asf(A)=1\))
      🔗
    Shears and Cavalieri’s Principle The most surprising \(\asf\) is that of the shear. This is a manifestation of Cavalieri’s principle, which implies that if we have a stack of rectangles, they cover the same area, regardless of whether they are perfectly stacked upright or if they are offset.
    🔗
    Play with Cavalieri’s Principle at GeoGebra ID: vrnff7nj.
    🔗

    Consider each of the following matrix products.
    🔗

    Use the Area Comparison Tool to find the \(\asf\) of each of the matrices in each product. How does the \(\asf\) of each of the constituent matrices relate to the \(\asf\) of their product?
    🔗
    Summarize your observations.
    🔗
    🔗
    A third fundamental observation about asf is:
    🔗
    Observation 3: The area scaling factor of a composition is the product of the area scaling factors. Symbolically, this means the \(\asf\) satisfies the product formula \(\asf(AB)=\asf(A)\asf(B)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Combining our new observations of the \(\asf\) of simple transformations together with the product formula, we may now use elementary matrix decompositions to compute \(\asf\text{!}\)
    🔗

    Example 8.1.11. Using an EMD to compute \(\asf\).

    Let \(A=\left[\begin{array}{rr}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{array}\right]\text{.}\) I will walk through how we can use an EMD for \(A\) together with the observations of this lesson, to compute \(\asf(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Row reduction.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{rr}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{array}\right] \textcolor{red}{\rowP{1}{2}} \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 2\\3\amp 1\end{array}\right] \textcolor{blue}{\rowA{2}{1}{-3}} \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 2\\0\amp -5\end{array}\right] \textcolor{green}{\rowS{2}{-\frac{1}{5}}} \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] . \end{equation*}
    2. Record row operations with elementary matrices.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \textcolor{green}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\0\amp -\frac{1}{5}\end{array}\right]} \textcolor{blue}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\-3\amp 1\end{array}\right]} \textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{array}\right]} A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] . \end{equation*}
    3. Socks and Shoes over.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \textcolor{red}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{array}\right]} \textcolor{blue}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\3\amp 1\end{array}\right]} \textcolor{green}{\left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\0\amp -5\end{array}\right]} \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] . \end{equation*}
    4. Use \(\asf\) of simple transformations and the product formula.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \asf(A) = \textcolor{red}{(1)} \textcolor{blue}{(1)} \textcolor{green}{(5)} (1) = 5. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Activity 8.1.12.

    What is the \(\asf\) of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=\left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp -3\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\-10\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 0\\-4\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}1\amp 5\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}7\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr}2\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{array}\right]? \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We now connect \(\asf\) to the area of parallelograms. Consider the parallelogram in the plane determined by the basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{\vect{v},\vect{w}\}\text{.}\) This parallelogram is the \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square \(S_\mathcal{B}\) (Figure Figure 8.1.13). The matrix \(P_{\mathcal{B}}\text{,}\) when viewed as a transformation, sends the standard unit square \(S\) to the \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square \(S_\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \asf(P_\mathcal{B}) = \frac{\Area(T_{P_\mathcal{B}}(S))}{\Area(S)} = \frac{\Area(S_\mathcal{B})}{\Area(S)} = \frac{\Area(S_\mathcal{B})}{1} = \Area(S_\mathcal{B}). \end{equation*}
    We have just justified the following proposition.
    🔗

    Proposition 8.1.14. Proposition (\(\mathcal{B}\)-unit squares and \(\asf\)).

    If \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2\}\subset \R^2\) is a basis, and \(S_\mathcal{B}\) is the \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square, then \(\asf(P_\mathcal{B})= \Area(S_\mathcal{B})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This proposition has many uses in geometry.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.15. Area of a Parallelogram.

    Let \(R\) denote the parallelogram determined by vectors \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then, using the computation in Example Example 8.1.11,
    \begin{equation*} \Area(R)=\asf\left(\left[\begin{array}{rr}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{array}\right]\right)=5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Observe how areas of parallelograms emerge from \(\asf\text{.}\) Note that all triangles are half of a parallelogram. As such, \(\asf\) gives us a way to compute areas of triangles! Just like the side lengths () and angle measures of triangle (), area of a triangle emerges from linear algebraic concepts.
    🔗
    We can now generalize our reasoning to \(\R^n\) for \(n\ge 2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.16. Unit Cube in \(\R^n\).

    The unit cube \(C_n\subset \R^n\) is the parallelepiped determined by the vectors \(\vect{e}_1,\vect{e}_2,\ldots, \vect{e}_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The volume of a region in \(\R^n\) is the size of a region as measured in units of the \(n\)-dimensional unit cube \(C_n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.18. Volume in \(\R^n\).

    If \(R\) is closed polygonal region in \(\R^n\text{,}\) its volume, denoted \(\Vol(R)\text{,}\) is the number of disjoint copies of the unit cube needed to fill \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    It is convention to talk about volume in dimensions \(n\ge 2\text{,}\) and just be aware that volume when \(n=2\) is our usual notion of area. All properties in Proposition Proposition 8.1.3 generalize to volume. For any \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{,}\) we can think about its associated volume scaling factor \(\vsf\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.19. Volume Scaling Factor (vsf).

    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix and \(R\) is any closed polygonal region, then the volume scaling factor of \(A\) is the constant value \(\vsf(A) = \frac{\Vol(T_A(R))}{\Vol(R)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Visualize volume scaling factor in \(\R^3\) at GeoGebra ID: xkapvwer.
    🔗
    From here on out, when considering all \(n\ge2\) we will use the term volume scaling factor and will only use the term area scaling factor when we are only looking at the plane.
    🔗
    Again, upon generalizing our reasoning to \(\R^n\text{,}\) we see \(\vsf\) connects to the volume of parallelepipeds.
    🔗

    Proposition 8.1.20. Proposition (\(\mathcal{B}\)-unit Cubes and \(\vsf\)).

    If \(\mathcal{B}\subset \R^n\) is an ordered basis and \(P_{\mathcal{B}}\) the change of basis matrix from \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates to standard coordinates, then the volume of the \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit cube is \(\Vol(C_{\mathcal{B}})=\vsf(P_{\mathcal{B}})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.1.21.

    Consider the parallelepiped \(R\) in \(\R^3\) determined by basis \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{a},\vect{b},\vect{c}\}\) where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vect{a}= \left[\begin{array}{r} 2\\-1\\1\end{array}\right], \vect{b}= \left[\begin{array}{r}0\\3\\1\end{array}\right], \text{ and } \vect{c}= \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    You may play with \(R\) at GeoGebra ID: vrmtn26v.
    🔗
    Then \(\Vol(R)= \vsf(P_\mathcal{B})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We see \(P_\mathcal{B}\) has an EMD:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 2\amp 0\amp 0\\ -1\amp 3\amp 0 \\ 1\amp 1\amp 1\end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 2\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1\amp 0\amp 0\\ -1\amp 1\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1\amp 0 \\ 1\amp 0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 3\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 0\amp 1\end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 1\amp 1\end{array}\right]. \end{equation*}
    Using the product formula: \(\vsf(A) = (2)(1)(1)(3)(1) = 6\text{.}\) We deduce the parallelepiped has volume 6! Without \(\vsf\text{,}\) how else could you have computed that?!

    Subsection Orientation

    A path is a loop if it closes up. A loop is simple if it does not intersect itself.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.23. Orientation in the Plane.

    If \(\ell\) is simple loop in the plane, then its orientation is the direction it travels. The two options are counterclockwise and clockwise.
    🔗
    🔗
    When looking at a matrix transformation of the plane, another natural question is: what happens to the orientation of loops? Do counterclockwise loops go to counterclockwise loops, or are they flipped to clockwise loops?
    🔗
    If orientation is preserved, we say \(A\) has positive sign and if orientation flipped, we say \(A\) has negative sign. We denote this by \(\sgn(A)=1\) or \(\sgn(A)=-1\text{,}\) respectively.
    🔗

    🔗
    🔗
    (b) Simple Transformations.
    How do you think each of the simple transformations: stretches, reflections, rotations, projections, and shears will affect orientation? For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\quad \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{bmatrix}\quad \begin{bmatrix}0\amp -1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix}\quad \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\quad \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    After writing down your expectations, use the Orientation Comparison Tool to check. Summarize your findings.
    🔗
    🔗
    (c) Compositions.
    Consider each of the following matrix products.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}-1\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}-1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{bmatrix} \quad\quad \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 2\\3\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    How does the \(\sgn\) of each of the constituent matrices relate to the \(\sgn\) of their product? Summarize your observations.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Just like for the \(\asf\text{,}\) you should make three fundamental observations:
    🔗
    Observation 1: Changing your simple loop does not affect the sign.
    🔗
    Observation 2: Simple transformations affect orientation in the following way.
    🔗
    Pure stretches do not affect orientation. (i.e., \(\sgn(A)=1\))
    🔗
    Projections smash curve to 0. (i.e., \(\sgn(A)\) is N/A)
    🔗
    Pure reflections reverse orientation. (i.e., \(\sgn(A)=-1\))
    🔗
    Pure shears do not affect orientation. (i.e., \(\sgn(A)=1\))
    🔗
    Pure rotations do not affect orientation. (i.e., \(\sgn(A)=1\))
    🔗
    Observation 3: The sign of a composition is the product of the signs. Symbolically, this means the \(\sgn\) satisfies the product formula \(\sgn(AB)=\sgn(A)\sgn(B)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Once more, we may use these observations with EMDs to compute the sign of any matrix.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.26. Using an EMD to compute \(\sgn\).

    If \(A=\begin{bmatrix}3\amp 1\\1\amp 2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then in Example 8.1.11 we computed
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \color{red}{\begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix}} \color{blue}{\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\3\amp 1\end{bmatrix}} \color{green}{\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\0\amp -5\end{bmatrix}} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sgn(A) = \color{red}{(-1)} \color{blue}{(1)} \color{green}{(-1)} (1) = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Activity 8.1.27.

    What is the sign of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} 1\amp -3\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\-10\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\0\amp -1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\-4\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 5\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 7\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0\amp 1\\1\amp 0\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2\amp 0\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}? \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Generalizing sign to \(\mathbb{R}^n\) for \(n \ge 3\) is slightly nuanced. We cannot just think about orientations of simple loops in \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\) (Why do you think that is?) However, in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{,}\) we found we could find the sign by counting reflections. It is this perspective that easily generalizes to \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.28. Sign.

    If \(A\) is an invertible \(n\times n\) matrix and \(r\) is the number of times a reflection appears in an elementary matrix decomposition of \(A\text{,}\) then its sign is \(\sgn(A)=(-1)^r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Different elementary matrix decompositions might have different numbers of reflections. It is a proposition to be proved elsewhere that the parity of this number only depends on \(A\text{,}\) not the elementary matrix decomposition, and hence \(\sgn(A)\text{,}\) as defined in Definition 8.1.28 is well-defined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Determinant

    The volume scaling factor and sign, while measuring different things, are deeply related and we can put them together into a single number.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.29. Determinant.

    The signed volume scaling factor of \(A\) is the product of its sign and volume scaling factor. This number is also called the determinant of \(A\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = \sgn(A)\operatorname{vsf}(A). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remark 8.1.30. Different Notations For Determinant.

    Recall in Notation that I represent matrices in three different ways: name, columns, and entries. Depending on the relevant representation of the matrix, I represent its determinant differently.
    🔗
    • Name: When working with a matrix name, I usually use “det.”
      🔗
      🔗
    • Column: When working with the columns of \(A\text{,}\) namely, \(A=[\mathbf{v}_1\mid \cdots\mid \mathbf{v}_n]\text{,}\) I will sometimes write the determinant of \(A\) in terms of its columns as \(\det(A)=\det(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n)\text{.}\) In this way, the determinant can then be viewed as a rule on columns, which turns out to be a productive perspective when working in multilinear algebra.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Entries: When I work with matrix entries, I often use vertical bars.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    For example, if \(A=[\mathbf{v}\mid\mathbf{w}]=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 2\\3\amp 4\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) then the three representations of the determinant are:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A)=\det(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=\begin{vmatrix}1\amp 2\\3\amp 4\end{vmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The determinant is an important number in linear algebra. In other textbooks, you will see it defined differently, often via formulas or algebraic properties. Here, I made the pedagogical decision to start with and center “intuitive” geometric properties to help give the number meaning and aid you in your reasoning about the determinant.
    🔗
    In Lesson 8.3, we will develop numerous strategies for computing the determinant, some of which you may already know. I want to remind you that is important to center the meaning of the determinant as signed volume scaling factor. In particular, \(|\det(A)|\) is \(\operatorname{vsf}(A)\text{.}\) The determinant arises in many formulas throughout math, and hence throughout STEM, and I want you to remember its geometric origins: when you see a determinant, there is a signed volume scaling factor nearby.
