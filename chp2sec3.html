    Lesson 2.3 Lines as Traveling Down a Path

    In this lesson, we use our understanding of vectors and linear combination to inform how we think about lines in the plane, and then generalize to how we think about lines (and planes) in \(\R^m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    Lines in the plane.
    🔗

    Subsection Lines in \(\R^2\)

    While I expect anyone taking this course to have seen lines before, it may be useful to remind you what they are and how we describe them. Lines are subsets of the plane satisfying certain geometric properties, and these subsets can be describe by certain equations.
    🔗

    Definition 2.3.1. Lines in \(\R^2\).

    A line \(\ell\) in the plane \(\R^2\) is the set of all points \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\in \R^2\) that satisfy an algebraic equation of the form \(px+qy=r\text{.}\) This is a normal equation of a line. Using set builder notation, I may write
    \begin{equation*} \ell = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \in \R^2 \ \mid \ px+qy=r\right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s take a moment to discuss the equations that describe the points on a line. Every line satisfies a normal equation. If the line is not vertical (i.e. \(q\ne 0\)), then its points satisfy two other algebraically equivalent equations:
    • Slope-Intercept Equation \(y=mx+b\text{,}\) where the line has slope \(m\) and \(y\)-intercept \(b\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Point-Slope Equation \(y=m(x-x_0)+y_0\text{,}\) where the line goes through point \((x_0,y_0)\) and has slope \(m\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    We can think of these equations as constraints that points in the plane must satisfy to lie on the given line.
    🔗
    Keep in mind, a line is a set of points, not one of the many equations that describe those points. Now recall that points in the plane may be understood as vectors. Can we use vectors to formulate new and productive ways to describe the points that comprise a line?
    🔗

    Example 2.3.2. Finding vectors that describe a line.

    Consider the line \(\ell\) with slope-intercept equation \(y=3x-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \((x,y)\) is the coordinate of a point on \(\ell\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}x\\3x-2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\-2\end{bmatrix} + x\begin{bmatrix}1 \\3\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Name \(\vect{p}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vect{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then I can think of this line \(\ell\) via a metaphor of traveling along a path as follows:
    1. \(\vect{p}\) is your starting position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\vect{v}\) is the direction and distance you travel in 1 unit of time (i.e. velocity).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(x\) is your elapsed time. When \(x\) is positive, you can think of \(\vect{p} + x\vect{v}\) as what your position on the path will be \(x\) units of time in the future, and when \(x\) is negative, you can think of \(\vect{p} + x\vect{v}\) as what your position had been \(x\) units of time in the past. Since \(x\) here is conceptualized as time, we often replace the symbol \(x\) with the symbol \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In symbols, the line can be expressed as the set of points \(\ell = \left\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\-2\end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix} \ \bigg| \ t\in \R \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    The graph of the line with equation \(y=3x-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We can generalize our reasoning in Example 2.3.2 to any line in the plane.
    🔗

    Proposition 2.3.3. Parametric Vector Form of Lines in \(\R^2\).

    If \(\ell\) is a line in \(\R^2\text{,}\) then there exists vectors \(\vect{v},\vect{p}\in \R^2\) such that \(\ell = \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v} \ \big| \ t\in \R\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    To begin, suppose \(\ell\) is not vertical and has slope-intercept equation \(y=mx+b\text{.}\) If \((x,y)\) are the coordinates of a point on \(\ell\text{,}\) then we may substitute for \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}x\\mx+b\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\b\end{bmatrix} + x\begin{bmatrix}1 \\m\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Name \(\vect{p}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\b\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vect{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\m\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \vect{p}+x\vect{v}\text{.}\) Since this is true for all \(x\in \R\text{,}\) it follows that \(\ell = \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v} \ \big| \ t\in \R\}\text{.}\) The case when \(\ell\) is vertical is left as an exercise.
    🔗
    🔗
    Observe the role substitution played in both Example 2.3.2 and Proposition 2.3.3. Using constraining equations and substituting in this way will be a reoccurring strategy throughout this course.
    🔗

    Example 2.3.4. Parametric to Slope-Intercept Conversion.

    Consider the path with initial position \(\vect{p}=\begin{bmatrix} 3\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and travels with velocity \(\vect{v}=\begin{bmatrix} -1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thinking of the scalar in front of \(\vect{v}\) as time, I mark off units of \(\vect{v}\) on the path, and I label the points at times \(-1\text{,}\) 0, 1, 2, and 3. As we range over all time, the collection of all points on the path can be expressed as
    \begin{equation*} \left\{\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+t\begin{bmatrix} -1\\2\end{bmatrix}\ \bigg| \ t\in \R \right\}. \end{equation*}
    In general, at time \(t\) my position on the path has coordinate \((x,y) = (3-t, 1+2t)\text{.}\) Since \(x=3-t\text{,}\) then \(t=3-x\text{.}\) Substituting this into the equation for \(y\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = 1+2(3-x) = -2x+7. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    
    The graph of the line with equation \(y= -2x+7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Once again, we can generalize our reasoning in Example 2.3.4 to any line in the plane.
    🔗

    Proposition 2.3.5.

    If \(\vect{v},\vect{p}\in \R^2\text{,}\) then the set \(\ell = \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v} \ \big| \ t\in \R\}\) is a line.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    To show that the set \(\ell\) is a line, I need to verify the coordinates satisfy an equation of a line ( Definition 2.3.1). Unpacking entries, \(\ell = \left\{\begin{bmatrix}p_1\\p_2\end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix}v_1 \\v_2\end{bmatrix}\ \bigg| \ t\in \R\right\}\text{.}\) If \((x,y)\) is the coordinate of a point on \(\ell\text{,}\) then \(x=p_1+tv_1\) and \(y=p_2+tv_2\text{.}\) If \(v_1\ne 0\text{,}\) then the first equations can be rewritten as \(t=\frac{1}{v_1}(x-p_1)\text{,}\) which can then be substituted into the second equation to get:
    \begin{equation*} y= p_2+ \frac{1}{v_1}(x-p_1)v_2 = \frac{v_2}{v_1}x + (p_2-p_1\frac{v_2}{v_1}). \end{equation*}
    This is the slope-intercept form of a line ( Definition 2.3.1). The case of \(v_1=0\) is left as an exercise.
    🔗
    🔗
    Revisit how in GeoGebra Activity 2.1.19, Part 5, as you fix \(r=1\) and slide \(s\text{,}\) the tips of the vectors \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+s \begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) sweeps out a line through \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and in the direction of \(\begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    🔗
    To summarize, you should reconceptualize lines as the collection of all points in the plane reached by traveling along a path in the following way:
    • Begin at a given starting point, symbolized as \(\vect{p}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Start traveling in a constant direction and constant speed, symbolized as \(\vect{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Your position on this path at time \(t\) can be represented with the linear combination \(\vect{p}+t\vect{v}\text{.}\)

    Subsection Lines in \(\R^m\)

    It is with this conceptualization of lines in the plane that we may generalize to reason about lines in \(\R^m\text{.}\) We formalize this in the following definition.
    🔗

    Definition 2.3.8. Lines in \(\R^m\) and their Parametric Vector Form.

    If \(\vect{p}, \vect{v}\in\R^m\) and \(\vect{v}\) is nonzero, then the set \(\ell:= \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v}\ \big| \ t\in \R\}\) is a line in \(\R^m\text{,}\) symbolized in parametric vector form. Here \(t\) is a parameter. If \([a,b]\subset \R\) is a closed interval, then \(s := \{\vect{p}+t\vect{v}\ \big| \ t\in [a,b]\}\) is a line segment in \(\R^m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.9. Lines as Paths of Travel.

    1. \(\vect{p}\) gives your starting position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\vect{v}\) gives the direction and distance travelled in 1 unit of time (i.e. velocity).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(t\) gives you elapsed time. When \(t\) is positive, you can think of \(\vect{p} + t\vect{v}\) as what your position will be \(t\) units of time in the future, and when \(x\) is negative, you can think of \(\vect{p} + t\vect{v}\) as what your position had been \(t\) units of time in the past.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    
    A line in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The line in Figure 2.3.10 is given by parametric vector form \(\ell = \left\{\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}+t\begin{bmatrix}-1\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix} \ \big| \ t\in \R \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection Planes in \(\R^m\)

    We now move on to generalize our reasoning to a new geometric object: a plane in \(\R^m\text{.}\) While many have a sense of what a plane is when they see it, it is another matter to make sense of it mathematically. I will provide a geometric formulation. If points, \(\vect{p}\text{,}\) \(\vect{a}\text{,}\) and \(\vect{b}\) in \(\R^m\) do not all lie on the same line (in the language of geometry language, are non-collinear), then they form a triangle. Emanating from vertex \(\vect{p}\) are sides \(\vect{v}:=\vect{a}-\vect{p}\) and \(\vect{w}:=\vect{b}-\vect{p}\text{.}\) The set of all points that I can reach by traveling with velocity vectors coming from the triangle’s sides is a plane. In a qualitative sense, the plane a triangle determines is the unique flat slice of \(\R^m\) that contains that triangle.
    🔗
    
    A plane in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.13. Plane through 3 non-collinear points.

    1. Start at position \(\vect{p}\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Travel with constant direction and speed \(\vect{v}:=\vect{a}-\vect{p}\) for \(s\) units of time,
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Travel with constant direction and speed \(\vect{w}:=\vect{b}-\vect{p}\) for \(t\) units of time.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.3.14. Non-Collinear Vectors.

    If \(\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^m\) are nonzero vectors and lines \(\ell_\vect{v}=\{t\vect{v}\ \big| \ t\in \R\}\) and \(\ell_\vect{w}=\{t\vect{w}\ \big| \ t\in \R\}\) are not equal, then \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\) are non-collinear vectors.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 2.3.15. Planes in \(\R^m\) and their Parametric Vector Form.

    If \(\vect{p},\vect{v},\vect{w}\in \R^m\) such that \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\) are non-collinear, then the set of points
    \begin{equation*} \rho=\left\{\vect{p}+s\vect{v}+t\vect{w} \ \big| \ s,t\in \R\right\}, \end{equation*}
    is a plane, symbolized in parametric vector form. Here \(s\) and \(t\) are parameters.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.16. Plane in \(\R^3\).

    Below is an image of the plane \(\rho\) in \(\R^3\) with parametric vector form:
    \begin{equation*} \rho=\left\{ \begin{bmatrix}5\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}+ s\begin{bmatrix}6\\6\\1\end{bmatrix}+ t\begin{bmatrix} -2\\3\\1\end{bmatrix}\ \bigg| \ s,t\in\R\right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    
    A plane in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the metaphor of travel, this plane is the collection of all points we can reach if we start at position \(\vect{p}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) travel \(s\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{v}=\begin{bmatrix}6\\6\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and then \(t\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{w}=\begin{bmatrix} -2\\3\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) For example, to reach the point \(\begin{bmatrix}4\\10\\4.5\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) we start at \(\vect{p}\text{,}\) travel \(\frac{1}{2}\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{v}\text{,}\) and then \(2\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{w}\text{.}\) This path is depicted in orange.
    🔗
    🔗

    Below is an image of the plane \(\mu\) in \(\R^2\) with parametric vector form:
    \begin{equation*} \mu = \left\{s\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+t\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\ \bigg| \ s,t\in\R\right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    
    A plane in \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the metaphor of travel, \(\mu\) is the collection of all points we can reach if we start at position \(\vect{p}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) travel \(s\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{v}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and then \(t\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{w}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Observe that \(\mu\) fills up all of \(\R^2\) - I can reach any point in \(\R^2\) by traveling for the appropriate amounts of time with constant velocities \(\vect{v}\) and \(\vect{w}\text{.}\) For example, to reach the point \(\vect{u}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) we start at the origin \(\vect{0}\text{,}\) travel \(0.4\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{v}\text{,}\) and then \(0.8\) units of time with constant velocity \(\vect{w}\text{.}\)
