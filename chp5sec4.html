    Lesson 5.4 Basis as Coordinate System

    A coordinate system is an agreed upon rule to describe where points are relative to us. We care about ordered bases because they give us a new coordinate system to describe things. As we will see later, problems that seem difficult when looking at them relative to the standard coordinates become simple when viewed relative to a different, well-chosen coordinate system.
    🔗

    Insight 5.4.1. Coordinatization.

    This book takes the perspective that objects exist external to, and independent of, us humans. Objects do not come to us with grid lines. Coordinatization is the mental act of imposing coordinates on objects to allow us to describe them, to analyze them, and to solve problems concerning them. Coordinatization is fundamentally human. We can and must choose coordinates. At first, this may seem like an extra burden, however it is in fact a powerful tool. When we confront a problem, we can and should reflect upon the situation and choose the coordinate system that enables us to most easily achieve our goals. This is what we do in calculus when we decide if we wish to approach the problem with Cartesian coordinates, polar coordinates, or something else altogether. This is the same thing we do in linear algebra.
    🔗
    🔗
    In linear algebra, it is bases that give us coordinates on subspaces.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.3. Coordinate Vector.

    If \(W\subset \mathbb{R}^m\) is a subspace, \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\subset W\) is a basis for \(W\text{,}\) \(\vect{w}\in W\text{,}\) and the collection of scalars \(x_1,\ldots x_n\in \mathbb{R}\) are the unique solution scalars for making \(\vect{w}\) with \(\mathcal{B}\text{,}\) then the \(x_i\) are the coordinates of \(\vect{w}\) in the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate system, and
    \begin{equation*} [\vect{w}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots\\ \ x_n\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate vector for \(\vect{w}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The Fundamental Theorem of Bases (Theorem 5.3.6) implies that any vector \(\vect{w}\in W\) has a unique \(\mathcal{B}\)-label.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.4. Standard Coordinates on \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

    The standard basis \(\mathcal{E}=\{\vect{e}_1,\ldots, \vect{e}_n\}\) gives the standard coordinates on \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\) If \(\vect{v}\in \mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \vect{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1\\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} = v_1\begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + v_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \cdots + v_n \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = v_1\vect{e}_1+v_2\vect{e}_2+\cdots + v_n\vect{e}_n \end{equation*}
    so \([\vect{v}]_{\mathcal{E}} = \vect{v}\text{.}\) The columns that we have been writing have been in standard coordinates this whole time!
    🔗
    🔗
    When conceptualizing column vectors as coordinates, or “labels,” the column \(\vect{w}\) and \([\vect{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}\) are different labels we assign to a fixed vector. Moving between these labels is what I will call change of coordinates or change of labels. If we write \(P_{\mathcal{B}}=[\vect{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \vect{v}_n]\text{,}\) then (using our understanding of Matrix Multiplication as Linear Combination Definition 3.1.5), we see
    \begin{equation*} \vect{w} = x_1\vect{v}_1+\cdots + x_n\vect{v}_n = \underbrace{[\vect{v}_1\mid\cdots\mid\vect{v}_n]}_{P_{\mathcal{B}}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\ \vdots \\ x_n\end{bmatrix}}_{[\vect{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplication by \(P_{\mathcal{B}}\) changes the \(\mathcal{B}\)-label to the standard label!!! We have previously conceptualized matrix multiplication as encoding (geometric) transformations and encoding row operations. Changing labels on a fixed vector is a fundamentally new and distinct conceptualization of matrix multiplication.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.5. Coordinate Change Matrix.

    If \(W\subset \mathbb{R}^m\) is a subspace and \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\ldots, \vect{v}_n\}\subset W\) is a basis, then \(P_{\mathcal{B}}=[\vect{v}_1\mid\ldots\mid\vect{v}_n]\) is the coordinate change matrix.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.4.6.

    While we usually use \(A=A_S\) to represent the matrix whose columns are \(S\text{,}\) when working in the context of bases, it is common to represent this matrix with \(P=P_S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2\}\subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is a basis. We will draw lines that help us visually measure vectors in units of \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\text{.}\) For any integer \(n\text{,}\) the \(n^{\text{th}}\) constant-\(\vect{v}_1\) line is the line with parametric vector form \(\vect{r}_n(t)= n\vect{v}_1+t\vect{v}_2\text{.}\) Similarly, we have constant-\(\vect{v}_2\) lines, reversing the role of \(n\) and \(t\text{.}\) The constant-\(\vect{v}_1\) lines and constant-\(\vect{v}_2\) lines fill up the plane and form the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate grid. The \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square is then the parallelogram determined by \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\text{.}\)

    Example 5.4.9.

    Let us return to the example of \(\mathcal{B}=\{\vect{v}_1, \vect{v}_2\}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}\) (Example 4.1.2). Let \(P_{\mathcal{B}}=[\vect{v}_1 \mid \vect{v}_2]\text{.}\) Since \(\operatorname{rref}(P_{\mathcal{B}})=I\text{,}\) Computational Quick Check Algorithm 5.3.11 implies \(\mathcal{B}\) is a basis.
    🔗
    To draw the constant-\(\vect{v}_1\) lines, I draw the sequence of lines:
    \begin{equation*} \vect{r}_0(t) = t\vect{v}_2, \quad \vect{r}_1(t) = \vect{v}_1+t\vect{v}_2, \quad \vect{r}_2(t) = 2\vect{v}_1+t\vect{v}_2, \quad \ldots, \quad \vect{r}_n(t) = n\vect{v}_1+t\vect{v}_2, \end{equation*}
    etc. for each integer \(n\text{.}\) In a similar way, I draw the sequence of constant-\(\vect{v}_2\) lines.
    🔗
    The \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate grid and \(\mathcal{B}\)-unit square are plotted to the right. I have also plotted the vector \(\vect{w}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\5\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Observe, it lies on both the standard and \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate grids, and we can read off its coordinates. Make sure you can see that in \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates, \([\vect{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Our previous computations have shown,
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1\amp-1\amp1\\1\amp1\amp5\end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1\amp0\amp3\\0\amp1\amp2\end{array}\right], \end{equation*}
    which symbolically corresponds to our visual observation above that \([\vect{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)

    Activity 5.4.13. The \(\mathcal{B}\)-Coordinate Grid Tool.

    In this activity, you will learn how to use GeoGebra to visualize the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate grid and use it to compute \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates. The GeoGebra functions you should gain proficiency with are: Line, Sequence, Coordinate, and Element. In so doing, you will create the B-Coordinate Grid Tool, which you will frequently revisit for the remainder of the course.
    🔗
    • Part 1: Creating your basis. Drop two moveable points \(A\) and \(B\) in the plane. Label the origin \(O\text{.}\) Use the Vector tool to draw the vector from \(O\) to \(A\) and then \(O\) to \(B\text{.}\) Name them \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\text{,}\) respectively. Move \(A\) and \(B\) so that \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\) are independent. You have now created your basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Part 2: Constructing grid lines part 1: One By One. The Line(P,Q) function creates a line going through the points \(P\) and \(Q\text{.}\) With that in mind, using the Line tool, create the first 3 constant-\(\vect{v}_1\) lines using the Line tool. (Think about what points will need to be input into Line and how do they relate to \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\)) Do the same for the first 3 constant-\(\vect{v}_2\) lines. You should now have a portion of your \(\mathcal{B}\)-grid!
      🔗
      🔗
    • Part 3: Constructing grid lines part 2: Sequence. When dealing with computers, whenever we encounter a thing we are doing over and over again, we want to “loop” that process so that the computer does all of the work in one line of code. GeoGebra does this with the Sequence function. More precisely you have to input 4 things: Sequence(Object(n), n, lower, upper), an object (such as a line) in terms of some value \(n\) which you want to vary, and then the lower and upper bound on those ranges. Use the Sequence function, together with what you learned in the previous part, to have a single line of GeoGebra code construct the first 21 constant-\(\vect{v}_1\) lines (10 in each direction). Do a similar thing for the first 21 constant-\(\vect{v}_2\) lines.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Part 4: Visualizing and Computing \(\mathcal{B}\)-Coordinates Drop a new moveable point \(C\) and draw the vector \(\mathbf{w}\) from \(O\) to \(C\text{.}\) The function x(A) picks out the \(x\)-coordinate of \(A\text{,}\) and similarly the function y(A) picks out the \(y\)-coordinate of \(A\text{.}\) Using these functions, input an augmented matrix whose solution set is the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates of \(\mathbf{w}\text{.}\) Use the ReducedRowEchelon command to reduce the matrix. You should see the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates here, but we need to pick them out. The Element function Element(M,i,j) picks out the entry in the \(i^{th}\) row and \(j^{th}\) column of the matrix \(M\) (e.g. Element(M,1,1) is the element of \(M\) in row 1 and column 1). Use Element to build the vector \([\mathbf{w}]_{\mathcal{B}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Part 5: Exploration
      Move around your basis and your vector \(\mathbf{w}\text{.}\) Visually estimate the \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates of \(\mathbf{w}\) and then check to see if your coordinate calculator agrees. When you are done playing, use your \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate tool to visually and symbolically analyze the following.

    More generally, if \(S\subset \mathbb{R}^m\) is a collection of \(n\)-many nonzero, linearly independent vectors, then \(S\) is a basis for its span. This gives a coordinate system on this span.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.15.

    Consider the collection \(S = \{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\}\subset \mathbb{R}^3\) where
    🔗
    \(\mathbf{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Let \(W= \mathrm{span}(S)\) be the plane spanned by \(S\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\) Every point on this plane can be uniquely described by the amount of \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and the amount of \(\mathbf{v}_2\text{.}\) This gives a coordinate grid on \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, consider the vector \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\0\\5 \end{bmatrix}\) lying in the plane \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since
    \begin{equation*} \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1&1&3\\2&-1&0\\1&2&5 \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1&0&1\\0&1&2\\0&0&0 \end{array}\right], \end{equation*}
    we have \([\mathbf{w}]_S=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Conversely, given \([\mathbf{u}]_S=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) this means \(\mathbf{u} = 2\mathbf{v}_1-\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\5\\0\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
