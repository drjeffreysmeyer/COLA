    Lesson 3.4 Composition

    If we have two transformations where the target of the first is the domain of the second, then we can chain them together and the output of the first becomes the input of the second.
    🔗
    
    A cartoon visualizing composition.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.1. Composition.

    If \(T_A:\mathbb{R}^m\to \mathbb{R}^n\) and \(T_B:\mathbb{R}^n\to \mathbb{R}^p\) are matrix transformations, then the composition of transformation \(T_A\) followed by transformation \(T_B\) is the new transformation
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{R}^m \xrightarrow{T_A} \mathbb{R}^n \xrightarrow{T_B} \mathbb{R}^p, \hspace{2pc} \text{where }\hspace{2pc} \vect{x} \xrightarrow{T_A} A\vect{x} \xrightarrow{T_B} B(A\vect{x}). \end{equation*}
    We denote this new rule \(T_B\circ T_A : \mathbb{R}^m\to \mathbb{R}^p\) and if \(\vect{x}\in\mathbb{R}^m\text{,}\) then \((T_B\circ T_A)(\vect{x}) = T_B(T_A(\vect{x}))=B(A\vect{x})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Observe that, for composition to make sense, the list-length of the output of the first transformation must be equal to the list-length of inputs for the second matrix transformation.
    🔗

    Warning 3.4.2.

    In the notation above, \(T_A\) is performed first, and \(T_B\) is performed second. In other words, since we are using function notation, we read the transformation from right to left!
    🔗
    🔗
    Composition is a very general concept which extends far beyond linear algebra. You will see it in calculus, analysis, geometry, and algebra, just to name a few places. However, if both \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are matrix transformations, i.e., there exist matrices \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) such that \(T_1(\vect{y})=A_1\vect{y}\) and \(T_2(\vect{x})=A_2\vect{x}\text{,}\) then the composition is also a matrix transformation!
    🔗

    Proposition 3.4.3. The composition of matrix transformations is a matrix transformation.

    If \(T_B(\vect{y})=B\vect{y}\) and \(T_A(\vect{x})=A\vect{x}\text{,}\) then \((T_B\circ T_A)(\vect{x}) = (BA)\vect{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Since matrix multiplication is associative (Proposition Proposition 3.1.17)
    \begin{equation*} (T_B\circ T_A)(\vect{x})=T_B(T_A(\vect{x}))=B(A\vect{x})=(BA)\vect{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.4.

    The product \(\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 6\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 2\\0\amp 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\0\amp 3\end{bmatrix}\) means first stretch by a factor of 2 in the \(x\)-direction and factor of 3 in the \(y\)-direction, and next shear the \(y\)-axis in the \(x\)-direction to the line \(y=\frac{1}{2}x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Observe that if you performed the composition in the opposite order, you would get a different transformation!
    🔗
    🔗
    Visualize composition as the product of matrices at GeoGebra ID: nq5kewqt.
    🔗

    Activity 3.4.8. Identifying Compositions.

    In this activity, you will expand upon GeoGebra Activity Technology 3.3.15, looking more carefully at certain special classes of matrices which correspond to compositions of simple transformations.
    🔗
    1. Setting it up again. Quickly rebuild your Transformation Visualizer Tool. This is good practice as this should only take a moment. This should include the unit square, a matrix \(A\text{,}\) and the transformed unit square.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Analyzing a new class of transformations. In this part, you will analyze the transformations given by the class of matrices of the form \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a\amp -b\\b\amp a\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Define two sliders \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) and within the Algebra Window, insert them in the appropriate places to create the matrix \(A\text{.}\) Start analyzing the transformed unit square. Try a few specific values such at \(a=1,b=-1\) and \(a=3,b=4\text{.}\) What do you see? Now as you vary \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) what types of transformations are you seeing? Can you identify these transformations as a composition of two simple geometric transformations (e.g., stretch, shear, reflect, rotate, and/or project)? Once you have some confidence in your description, carefully record it using geometric words in complete sentences.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Analyzing a new class of transformations. Repeat Part 2 for matrix \(B=\begin{bmatrix}c\amp 1\\0\amp c\end{bmatrix}\) and slider \(c\text{.}\) Start with values \(c=1\text{,}\) then \(c=2\text{,}\) and then go from there.
      🔗
      🔗

    Insight 3.4.9. Decomposition as a problem solving strategy.

    A major goal of future lessons (see, for example, Chapter 7) is to decompose a complicated matrix into a product of simpler matrices, thereby allowing us to identify its associated transformation as a composition of simple transformations.
