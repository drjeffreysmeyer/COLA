    Lesson 10.1 Vector Size

    The motivating question of this lesson is: What is the "size" of a vector? By size, here I mean an absolute size, single number that captures the "bigness" of the vector. This is in contrast to previous chapters, where we looked at relative size of two vectors (i.e. \(2\vect{v}\) is two times larger than \(\vect{v}\)). There are many reasonable ways to measure size, and we will begin by investigating them in a variety of contexts.
    🔗

    Quantitative Situation 10.1.1. Size in Quantitative Situations.

    Revisit Quantitative Situation 1.1.4. What would you say is the size of that vector of travel directions? What if you were a bird?
    🔗
    Revisit Quantitative Situation 1.1.6. What would you say is the size of that vector of temperatures?
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 10.1.2. Size in \(\R^2\).

    Here we investigate the size of each vector in \(S= \{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2,\vect{v}_3\} = \left\{ \left[\begin{array}{r}-2\\2\end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{r}3\\3\end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{r}-4\\1\end{array}\right]\right\}.\)
    🔗
    Discuss relevant ways to measure the size of each vector. Can you come up with multiple ways? Given a reasonable way to determine size, which vectors are the largest? Smallest?
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 10.1.3. Size in \(\R^3\).

    Investigate the size of each vector in \(S= \{\vect{v}_1,\vect{v}_2,\vect{v}_3\} = \left\{ \left[\begin{array}{r}6\\0\\0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}{r}1\\5\\5\end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{r}4\\4\\4\end{array}\right]\right\}.\) Make an argument why each vector could be considered to have the largest size. (Note: each of them has a reasonable argument - think it through!)
    🔗
    🔗
    As you may have seen in the previous activities, there are many reasonable ways to measure size. I will outline a few here, but note that you may think of other ways not listed below.
    🔗
    Recall the forecast from Quantitative Situation 1.1.6. It would be reasonable to say that the "size" of this forecast vector is the highest temperature expected, in which case, its "size" would be \(76^{\circ}\) F.
    🔗
    More generally, one reasonable way to measure the size of a vector \(\vect{v}\) is by the magnitude of its largest entry, which we will call its absolute maximum.
    🔗

    Definition 10.1.4. Absolute Maximum (\(\ell^\infty\)-Norm).

    If \(\vect{v}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then its absolute maximum, or \(\ell^\infty\)-norm, is the largest magnitude of its entries. In algebraic symbols, \(\norm{\vect{v}}_\infty= |v_i|\) where \(|v_i|\ge |v_j|\) for all \(j\in \{1,\ldots, n\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    When looking at a vector as a signal or data, it is often useful to think about its absolute maximum.
    🔗
    Recall the travel instructions from Quantitative Situation 1.1.4. Since you are restricted to move along streets, it would be reasonable to say the size of this vector is number of blocks you have to travel, in which case the "size" of this vector is 4. More generally, a second reasonable way to measure the size of a vector \(\vect{v}\) is by the sum of the magnitude of its entries, which we will call its absolute sum.
    🔗

    Definition 10.1.5. Absolute Sum (\(\ell^1\)-Norm).

    If \(\vect{v}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then its absolute sum, or \(\ell^1\)-norm, is the sum of the magnitude of its entries. In algebraic symbols, \(\norm{\vect{v}}_1= |v_1|+\cdots+|v_n|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The absolute sum has many interesting applications. It is related to the notion of Taxi Cab Geometry, where you consider the number of blocks traveled by a taxi cab to reach its destination. The absolute sum is also related to the average of the vector entries, which has many statistical applications.
    🔗
    A third reasonable way to measure the size of a vector \(\vect{v}\) is by its Euclidean length. To work with Euclidean length in \(\R^n\text{,}\) we will need to develop the Generalized Pythagorean Theorem.
    🔗
    We begin with the Pythagorean Theorem in the Euclidean plane, the classical result which states that, given a right triangle \(\triangle ABC\text{,}\) the area of the square off of the hypotenuse \(S_3\) is the sum of the areas of the squares off the legs (\(S_1\) and \(S_2\)):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Area(S_1) + \Area(S_2) = \Area(S_3), \end{equation*}
    yielding the famous equality,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a^2 + b^2 = c^2, \end{equation*}
    and hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}. \end{equation*}
    Identifying a vector \(\vect{v} = \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \in \R^2\) with the hypotenuse of the triangle, we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Length of }\vect{v} = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}. \end{equation*}
    By performing the Pythagorean Theorem twice, we get the Pythagorean Theorem in Euclidean 3-Space. We can iterate this process over and over again to find length in \(\R^n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 10.1.6. Length (\(\ell^2\)-Norm).

    If \(\vect{v}\in \R^n\text{,}\) then its length, or \(\ell^2\)-norm, is its Euclidean length (via the Generalized Pythagorean Theorem). In algebraic symbols, \(\norm{\vect{v}}_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots + v_n^2}.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 10.1.7.

    You may be asking, why call absolute magnitude, absolute sum, and length \(\ell^p\) (read ``ell p’’) where \(p\) is "infinity," "one," or "two?" In fact, these are instances of a broad family of ways of measuring size, or "norms," \(\norm{\vect{v}}_p:=(|v_1|^p+\cdots+|v_n|^p)^{\frac{1}{p}}\text{,}\) where \(p\in (0,\infty]\text{.}\) These norms play an important role in areas of math that build on linear algebra. In this course, we will only need the three I mentioned above. Furthermore, the \(\ell^2\)-norm (i.e. length) will be most important for us going forward, so unless mentioned otherwise, I will always use \(\norm{\vect{v}}\) (i.e. no subscript) to denote the \(\ell^2\)-norm \(\norm{\vect{v}}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.1.8.

    If \(\vect{v}=\left[\begin{array}{r}1\\-4\\2\\-2\end{array}\right]\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    • \(\norm{\vect{v}}_\infty = \max (1,4,2,2) = 4\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • \(\norm{\vect{v}}_1 = 1+4+2+2 = 9\text{,}\) and
      🔗
    • \(\norm{\vect{v}}_2 = \sqrt{1^2+4^2+2^2+2^2}= \sqrt{1+16+4+4} = 5\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗

    Activity 10.1.9.

    Revisit Activity 10.1.3. Order the vectors according to each of the three norms.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 10.1.10. Identify Sets of Norm 1.

    In the plane, clearly draw and label each of the following sets of points:
    🔗
    1. The set of points that have absolute maximum of 1. In symbols \(C_\infty:=\{\vect{v} \ | \ \norm{\vect{v}}_\infty = 1\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. The set of points that have absolute sum of 1. In symbols \(C_1:=\{\vect{v} \ | \ \norm{\vect{v}}_1 = 1\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. The set of points that have length of 1. In symbols \(C_2:=\{\vect{v} \ | \ \norm{\vect{v}}_2 = 1\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Conjecture the shapes of the sets of norm 1 in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 10.1.11. Scaling a vector scales its norm.

    If \(\vect{v}\in\R^n\text{,}\) \(p\in \{\infty,1,2\}\text{,}\) and \(k \in \R\text{,}\) then \(\norm{k \vect{v}}_p = |k|\ \norm{\vect{v}}_p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We often care about the "direction" and not the norm of a vector. In such cases, we can use Proposition 10.1.11 to scale out by the norm and get a "norm 1" vector. In certain contexts, working with a norm 1 vector is much nicer than working with messy or large norms. The process of scaling a nonzero vector to length 1 is normalization (i.e. \(\vect{v} \mapsto \frac{1}{\norm{\vect{v}}} \vect{v}\)).
    🔗

    Definition 10.1.12. Unit Vector.

    A vector of length 1 is a unit vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    I will use the "hat" notation to denote the unit vector in the \(\vect{v}\)-direction, i.e. \(\widehat{\vect{v}} := \frac{1}{\norm{\vect{v}}} \vect{v}\text{.}\)
